Inspired by the Ka/Ks ratio, CKI (Cell-type Ka/Ks-inspired Index) decomposes transcriptomic divergence into a baseline rate k_n (housekeeping genes) and a functional rate k_f (identity genes); ω = k_f/k_n quantifies baseline-normalized functional divergence. In ground-truth simulation, ω rejected neutral housekeeping drift (false-positive rate 0.00 versus 0.55–0.58 for raw JS and cosine) and ranked first for functional-versus-neutral discrimination (AUC = 0.80). On real technical replicates, ω misreported none of 30 Kang IFN-β cross-lane pairs and had the lowest false-report rate among continuous divergence metrics on 2,161 brain library pairs (28.6% versus 45.2% for raw JS), though this specificity decays with group size. In 3,563 TCGA samples across five cancer types, ω revealed a consistent pan-cancer reversal—tumor specimens less divergent than adjacent non-tumor tissue (ratio 1.13–2.46, CI excluding 1 in four of five cancers)—driven by a 2.1–3.6-fold elevated housekeeping baseline, not reduced functional divergence; in lung adenocarcinoma, KRAS-mutant tumors showed elevated tissue-level divergence whose functional (k_f) and baseline (k_n) components both survived adjustment for tumor purity and smoking, whereas the apparent EGFR-mutant association was attributable to stromal/immune admixture. Brain analysis revealed a 6.10-fold regional gradient (size-balanced estimate 1.74 [1.64, 1.84]). CKI is freely available as an open-source Python package.

This question mirrors one addressed in molecular evolution. The Ka/Ks ratio (dN/dS) distinguishes nonsynonymous changes (Ka, which alter the protein) from synonymous changes (Ks, largely silent) 6,7, using synonymous sites as an internal baseline so that the ratio reveals selection. While CKI does not share Ka/Ks’s formal mathematical properties (notably the shared mutation rate that cancels in the ratio; see Discussion), it adopts an analogous heuristic logic for transcriptomic comparisons.
--- [results/CKI_Manuscript_NC_fulltext.txt line 13] ---
CKI defines two rates: a baseline divergence rate k_n, estimated from housekeeping (HK) gene expression, and a functional divergence rate k_f, estimated from cell-type identity genes. The ratio ω = k_f/k_n quantifies baseline-normalized functional divergence: values close to the equivalent-population calibration baseline (established empirically within each dataset; see Results) indicate baseline-consistent differences, values far above indicate functional divergence exceeding baseline variation, and values far below indicate strong functional constraint. The scope of ω is bounded by a single central assumption, stated here once and quantified empirically throughout: the HK anchor is valid only where housekeeping expression remains constrained. Functional signal located on HK genes is invisible to ω by construction, and in disease or strong-perturbation contexts the anchor itself shifts—housekeeping dysregulation and composition change in cancer (TCGA, below), IFN-β stimulation of housekeeping genes in the perturbation demonstration (below; simulated adversarial scenarios in Supplementary Note 1)—inflating the denominator and deflating the ratio. In such regimes ω must be read alongside its components, and k_f with a design-matched null is the honest default for ordering claims (Results; limitations are addressed in the Discussion). CKI ω is a heuristic index, not a formal measure of Darwinian selection; interpretation is anchored in the empirical distribution of ω within each analysis rather than in fixed ratio cut-offs or claims about selection regimes (see Discussion).

Here, we show that CKI provides a baseline-normalized index of cell-state divergence across five scales. First, we calibrated CKI on Tabula Muris mouse data 8: random splits of the same cell population yield ω above 1 (empirical calibration baseline 7.70, 95% CI [7.37, 8.02]). Second, in Tabula Sapiens human data 9, CKI ω is negatively correlated with all four standard distance metrics; a decomposition analysis showed this association is partly driven by the k_n denominator (k_n is itself strongly positively correlated with the standard metrics), so ω is a composite of numerator and denominator information rather than a fully independent dimension (see Results). Third, on real technical replicates—30 same-donor cross-lane pairs in an IFN-β PBMC cohort and 2,161 same-donor, same-region library pairs in the brain atlas—ω showed the lowest drift misreporting among continuous divergence metrics while retaining sensitivity to genuine biology (Results). Fourth, applied to TCGA cancer data 10,11, CKI mapped tissue-level functional divergence across five cancer types and 3,563 samples, uncovering a consistent pan-cancer reversal—tumor specimens less divergent than adjacent non-tumor tissue, attributable to an elevated housekeeping baseline—and resolving driver-class differences in lung adenocarcinoma, where KRAS-mutant tumors retained both functional (k_f) and baseline (k_n) divergence after purity and smoking adjustment, whereas the apparent EGFR association dissolved under purity adjustment (Results). Fifth, in a human brain single-nucleus atlas 12, we measured how non-neuronal cell classes differ across brain regions, quantifying a 6.10-fold regional differentiation gradient and screening for anomalously similar cell-type/region-pair candidates; because no candidate survived false-discovery-rate correction under a strict block-shuffle null preserving the cell-type-by-region design, we present the brain candidates as hypothesis-generating signals for future lineage-tracing validation. The human, pan-cancer, and brain analyses are complemented by mouse Tabula Muris calibration and ground-truth simulation analyses throughout.

Step 1: Compute the baseline divergence rate k_n. We restrict the pseudobulk vectors to housekeeping (HK) gene indices and add a +1 pseudo-count followed by L1 normalization; k_n is the JS divergence between the two HK-gene probability distributions. Because HK genes should not differ systematically between biologically equivalent populations 13, k_n captures baseline noise: technical variation, stochastic bursting, and individual-level physiological differences.

We performed control comparisons in which we randomly split the same cell population into two halves, across six FACS control populations (Fig. 2a). The initial calibration used one split per population (six control comparisons; mean ω 6.67, median 6.46, CV ≈ 52%), indicating substantial baseline variability. Repeating split-half 50 times per population (300 ω values) stabilized the baseline at mean ω = 7.70 (SD of replicate means 1.15, 95% CI [7.37, 8.02]); the legacy six-split estimate (6.67) is consistent, the difference attributable to single-split noise. No control comparison reached significance (all P > 0.05, one-sided permutation test): CKI recognizes biologically equivalent populations as having no functional divergence—though formal equivalence testing (TOST) would give stronger evidence; the 50-replicate calibration now bounds the precision of the calibration factor (ω_cal = ω / 7.70).

Because the empirical baseline (ω = 7.70, 95% CI [7.37, 8.02]; 50 split-half replicates across six control populations) deviates substantially from the theoretical ideal (ω = 1), we introduce a calibrated omega, ω_cal = ω_obs / 7.70, reported to at most two significant figures as order-of-magnitude estimates. Under this calibration the brain global mean becomes ≈ 5 (raw 38.55), astrocytes—the most regionally divergent class—≈ 11 (raw 82.75), and the most constrained classes ≈ 1.8 (raw 13.56). Scheme-matched split-half calibration inside the brain atlas (29 populations; B = 50 splits) gave an internal baseline of 9.73 (95% CI [9.03, 10.53]), ~1.3-fold above the mouse-derived factor, so the mouse calibration overstates brain ω_cal (brain-internal: global mean ≈ 4.0, astrocytes ≈ 8.5, Bergmann glia ≈ 1.4). In Tabula Sapiens the internal baseline was 7.67 (95% CI [7.39, 8.00]), inside the mouse-derived CI: the mouse factor transfers to Tabula Sapiens but not to the brain atlas—ω_cal is dataset-relative, not universal. Class-specific split-half baselines from the same 29 populations (range 7.58–12.52, a 1.65-fold spread) address the residual between-class heterogeneity: under per-class baselines the astrocyte-to-Bergmann-glia gradient is 5.99 (95% CI [4.43, 7.69] under a studentized bootstrap-t region-clustered bootstrap, which attains nominal coverage at 6–7 clusters where the earlier percentile interval [4.12, 9.18] under-covers; Supplementary Note 2), essentially unchanged (the two classes’ baselines nearly coincide, 9.26 vs 9.08), while calibrated levels compress (astrocytes ω_cal = 8.9; Bergmann glia ω_cal = 1.49, joint 95% CI [0.99, 2.12], no longer excluding 1). With the same correction, Bergmann glia’s class-mean CI widens to [5.76, 28.59] (was [8.49, 19.52]): the claim that Bergmann glia diverges above its class baseline (9.08) does not survive and is downgraded to a qualitative statement—cross-region ω is elevated in absolute terms but not separable from baseline at 7 regions (Supplementary Note 3). A ratio-estimator audit confirms the calibration absorbs ratio bias: under the split-half null E[k_f/k_n] exceeds E[k_f]/E[k_n] by a median +0.2% per class × region group (worst bin k_n < 10⁻⁴: +6.5%), and the gradient is robust to the summary statistic (6.10 mean, 6.00 median, 6.09 trimmed) and to excluding near-zero-k_n pairs (Supplementary Note 4). We recommend per-class calibration for within-dataset class contrasts; calibrated values accompany raw ω in all key results (Supplementary Fig. 2), and calibrate_omega() is available in the CKI package.
--- [results/CKI_Manuscript_NC_fulltext.txt line 30] ---
We computed CKI ω and four standard metrics (raw JS divergence, Spearman distance, cosine distance, marker Jaccard distance) on all 4,851 human cell-type pairs. CKI ω was negatively correlated with all four standard metrics (Spearman r = −0.36 to −0.46, all P < 0.001), whereas the four standard metrics formed a tight positive cluster (pairwise r = 0.57–0.93). Because ω = k_f/k_n is a ratio, a negative correlation can arise mechanically when the denominator k_n is itself positively correlated with those metrics. Decomposing the association: k_f was positively correlated with the standard metrics (r = +0.43 to +0.72) and k_n more strongly so (r = +0.69 to +0.81), and conditional on k_n the correlation between ω and each standard metric became positive in all four cases (partial r = +0.11 to +0.54, all P < 1 × 10⁻¹⁴). The negative raw correlation is thus partly a ratio artifact of the k_n denominator: ω combines k_f and k_n into a composite signal whose value lies in normalizing functional divergence against a population-specific baseline rather than in statistical independence from existing metrics.

CKI showed moderate cell-type classification performance (AUC = 0.680, ranked 5th of 5 methods; Table 1). The pair-level bootstrap 95% CI ([0.609, 0.745], B = 2,000) widens to [0.540, 0.804] under cell-type-entry clustering: the classification advantage is weak and resampling-unit dependent. The lower ranking is expected by design: CKI down-weights shared HK patterns to isolate functional divergence, trading global-identity sensitivity for identity-gene divergence. Critically, CKI was the only metric where same-organ different-cell-type pairs had higher values than different-organ different-cell-type pairs (mean ω 24.87, n = 1,038 vs. 20.80, n = 3,754; Mann-Whitney U, P < 0.001); all four standard metrics showed the opposite pattern. The reversal therefore indicates a more stable housekeeping baseline within organs, not greater functional specialization of same-organ cell types.

No real dataset provides pairs with known functional divergence, so we built a semi-synthetic ground truth by injecting known perturbations into a real background (Tabula Muris FACS marrow B cells; both groups resampled from the same cell type, so true pre-injection divergence is zero; Methods). Under pure neutral housekeeping drift, raw JS and cosine exceeded their null thresholds in 55% and 58% of replicates, whereas ω produced none (2% under global overdispersion; pooled neutral replicates: ω 2 of 250, Clopper-Pearson 95% CI [0.001, 0.029]; raw JS 0.553 [0.470, 0.635]; cosine 0.580 [0.497, 0.660]). Conversely, detecting an injected functional module required strong signals: at δ = 1 (twofold shift on 200 genes) ω detected none of 150 replicates (k_f alone 2; raw JS 99%; cosine 67%); only at δ = 2 did ω reach 13%. This insensitivity is structural: per-pair top-200 selection saturates with noise even under the null (median baseline k_f = 0.025), so weak shifts do not lift k_f above the selection floor—simulation-level confirmation that k_f is an upper-bound estimator. When signal and neutral drift co-occurred (δ = 0.5, η = 0.25–1), ω detection was further suppressed by the inflated denominator, whereas cosine and raw JS rose from 14% to 100% because they count neutral drift as divergence. Because the neutral-drift null is itself HK-defined—CKI’s own anchoring assumption—two adversarial scenarios quantify the boundary (Supplementary Note 1). With the module placed on HK genes, ω detected none (rate 0.000 at every δ ≥ 0.25; k_n itself fired at 0.61–1.00): ω is structurally blind to functional change on its anchor genes. Conversely, under expression-matched low-variance non-HK drift (N1 control), ω stayed at its calibrated false-positive rate (0.000–0.067 across η) while raw JS and cosine inflated to 0.81–1.00 at moderate-to-strong drift: the ratio cancels global compositional drift wherever it acts, so ω’s specificity is not an HK-anchoring artifact (Supplementary Note 5).

Integrating specificity and sensitivity, ω best discriminated functional (δ ≥ 0.25) from neutral perturbations (AUC = 0.80, versus 0.72 for k_f alone, 0.64 for raw JS, 0.58 for cosine, and 0.21 for k_n alone; bootstrap 95% CI 0.804 [0.777, 0.831], the advantage over k_f holding in 100% of resamples): the apparent power of the standard metrics is purchased with false positives on neutral drift. The ratio construction also conferred robustness to technical asymmetry: under a fourfold cell-count imbalance, ω changed by −32% while k_f inflated by +67% and cosine by +108%, and neither 30% dropout nor a twofold depth difference produced a systematic shift. These results delimit the intended use of ω: a specificity-first screen whose construction rejects neutral drift, at the cost of bounded power for weak-to-moderate functional signals (Supplementary Note 1). That power bound is background-dependent: the second background below has a lower k_f selection floor (median baseline k_f 0.011 versus 0.025), and at δ = 1 ω detection reached 0.91 there versus 0.00 in marrow.

The same design scales to the brain atlas, where 606 10x libraries are nested within (donor, region) combinations, yielding a four-tier drift ladder of library-level pairs within each cell type: same-library splits are embedded in the per-pair null; T1 pairs two libraries of the same donor, region, and cell type (pure technical drift; 2,161 pairs); T2 crosses donors within a region (technical plus inter-individual drift; 1,089 pairs); T3 crosses regions within a donor (regional biology; positive control; 1,656 pairs) 12. On T1, ω had the lowest misreporting rate among the four continuous divergence metrics (FPR 28.6%, CI [26.8, 30.6], versus 45.2% for raw JS, 44.1% for cosine, 40.6% for Spearman distance, 37.6% for k_f alone, and 35.7% for the baseline component k_n; Fig. 4b, c), and ω was below raw JS in 10 of 10 cell classes (below cosine in 9 of 10, below Spearman in 8 of 10; per-class raw-JS-to-ω FPR ratios 1.2–2.3). Marker Jaccard distance was lower still (T1 FPR 19.9%, below ω in all ten cell classes; T2 74.7% versus 90.9% for ω): judged on the false-positive statistic alone, it separates the tiers at least as well as ω (T3−T1 FPR gap 0.60 versus 0.56; T3/T1 ratio 4.0 versus 3.0), so the misreporting claim for ω is restricted to the continuous divergence metrics. Its advantage over marker Jaccard lies elsewhere: Jaccard responded weakest to genuine regional divergence (T3 calibration ratio 1.41 versus 1.80 for ω and 2.98 for raw JS) and, as a single set-overlap statistic, offers no k_n/k_f decomposition—the component split that localizes the TCGA reversal and the LUAD driver contrast (below)—so ω occupies the better trade-off between drift specificity, sensitivity to biology, and interpretability. At T2 the picture is not immunity but relative calibration: all metrics fire on most pairs (donor differences contain real biology), and ω again misreported least among the continuous divergence metrics (90.9% versus 98.4–98.7% for raw JS and k_f; calibration ratio 1.76 versus 3.32 and 3.22). At T3, every metric rose (ω calibration 1.80; raw JS 2.98), confirming that ω retains sensitivity to regional biology—across the ladder the ω calibration gradient is the shallowest of all metrics (1.04 → 1.76 → 1.80, versus 1.07 → 3.32 → 2.98 for raw JS), so ω best separates technical drift from biology in relative terms, not absolutely.

Two honest qualifications temper the brain result. First, the absolute ω FPR is not zero, and it grows with group size—from 14.1% for pairs with at most 30 nuclei per library to 48.0% for pairs exceeding 500 (raw JS from 28.6% to 72.5%)—indicating a real library-level technical component (capture efficiency, depth) that grows detectable with power and that the k_n denominator absorbs only partially: on T1 the k_n calibration itself sits at 1.05, so the housekeeping anchor removes most but not all of the drift. The Kang cross-lane replicates, with at least 50 cells per group, sit toward the small end of this size range, where misreporting is lowest—the 0-of-30 Kang outcome and the 28.6% brain T1 rate are therefore consistent under the size-dependence of the technical component. Second, a minority of cell classes show gene-specific library effects that the anchor cannot absorb: choroid plexus (9 pairs, median calibration 2.31) and Bergmann glia (27 pairs, 1.16) exceed the null most strongly—in choroid plexus, two libraries of the same donor, region, and type differ in k_f by up to 137-fold. The ladder thereby also quantifies the library-level structure that the block-shuffle null retains by design (Methods): the Bergmann-glia block-shuffle null mean (21.9) lying far above its split-half baseline (9.7) reflects precisely this within-donor, within-region technical divergence. The simulation-level claim of absolute neutral-drift immunity thus transfers to real data only as a relative-calibration advantage—lowest misreporting among the continuous divergence metrics (raw JS, cosine, Spearman, k_f) at every tier, with sensitivity to genuine regional divergence preserved (Fig. 4; audit trails in the companion repository; Section 3.12 of the Supplementary Information).

How ω behaves on a real perturbation whose effect on the housekeeping anchor is unknown was tested on peripheral blood mononuclear cells from eight donors, control versus 6-hour IFN-β stimulation 14 (24,413 cells, six cell types; pseudobulk per donor and condition; Methods). Because the two conditions were captured in separate 10x lanes, condition is fully confounded with lane for every metric; we therefore read the analysis as a relative architecture demonstration, not an absolute detection claim. Under that reading, the perturbation was visible at the ω level in all six cell types: median within-donor stimulated-versus-control ω exceeded median donor-versus-donor ω by 1.1–2.0-fold (1.8–3.4-fold above the split-half baseline); the within-donor permutation test reached raw P < 0.05 in 15 of 37 tests (no multiplicity correction; the aggregate excess over the ~2 of 37 expected under the global null is informative, individual tests are not). However, k_f alone separated perturbation from donor drift as well or better (rank AUC: ω 0.55–0.92, k_f 0.74–1.00, raw JS 0.79–1.00); in CD14+ monocytes ω AUC fell to 0.55 while k_f retained 0.98. The components are consistent with the anchor-visibility mechanism—stimulation raises housekeeping expression itself (median k_n rises 1.2–5.7-fold)—so the denominator inflates and partially cancels the functional signal, most strongly in monocytes. The demonstration thus supports, within a lane-confounded design, the anchor-visibility boundary in the decision rules (when the perturbation touches the anchor, the ratio deflates and k_f-only with a design-matched null is the more honest statistic), while ω tracks real perturbation above donor drift when the anchor is unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Note 6).

We benchmarked CKI against MELD (v1.0.2) and scDist on the Kang IFN-β dataset 14 (GEO: GSE96583); because scDist is R-only, we implemented a Python approximation (per-PC condition fixed-effect regression with donor covariates; distance = √Σβ²λ), labelled as such below. CKI and MELD agreed on effect direction in 6 of 6 cell types; MELD’s within-type separation was near-saturated (AUC 0.997–0.9998), leaving no gradient to compare, and per-type rankings were effectively unrelated (ρ ≤ 0.77): direction agrees, ordering does not. In an additive mean-shift simulation (fold changes of 2, 4, and 8 on 100 or 500 genes; 20 replicates), MELD and the scDist approximation detected every configuration (sensitivity 1.00; false-positive rates ≤ 0.05), whereas CKI ω rose from AUC 0.52 to 0.79 as the shift grew on 100 genes but collapsed to 0.05–0.13 on 500 genes—at 500 shifted genes the anchor k_n itself responds (k_n AUC = 1.000) and the ratio is annihilated by its denominator. ω therefore measures divergence in excess of the anchor and is, by design, insensitive to perturbations that move the anchor itself; users seeking maximal detection power for broad perturbations should use MELD, scDist, or CKI’s own k_f component. CKI’s working range is also bounded: donor-paired power falls from 0.67–0.93 at 50 cells per donor per condition to ≈ 0 at 500 and above (operating window ~50–200 cells; limitations are addressed in the Discussion).

We applied CKI to TCGA bulk RNA-seq data across five cancer types (LUAD, LUSC, LIHC, KIRC, BRCA) 10,11, totalling 3,563 samples, and asked how tissue-level functional divergence is reorganized when cancer develops. Because bulk RNA-seq averages over tumor, stromal, and immune compartments, ω here quantifies divergence between tissue states rather than between cell types; we therefore compare tumor–tumor (TT), normal–normal (NN), and tumor–normal (TN) pairs within each cancer type (Fig. 5; Supplementary Fig. 4). A consistent pan-cancer reversal emerged: in all five cancer types, NN pairs were more divergent than TT pairs—the ratio of mean NN to mean TT ω ranked LUAD 2.46 (95% CI 2.13–2.86; sample-level cluster bootstrap, B = 1,000), KIRC 1.90 (1.65–2.17), LUSC 1.82 (1.46–2.25), BRCA 1.57 (1.35–1.83), and LIHC 1.13 (0.97–1.34)—with the bootstrap CI excluding 1 in four of five cancers (LIHC CI including 1, direction consistent in all five; pair-level Mann-Whitney P-values, which ignore dyadic dependence between pairs, are reported as descriptive only in Section 3.13 of the Supplementary Information). Component decomposition identified the mechanism: in every cancer type the housekeeping baseline was elevated in tumors—TT k_n exceeded NN k_n by 2.1–3.6-fold at the median (mean-ratio 95% CIs excluding 1 in all five cancers, e.g. KIRC 3.21 [2.51, 4.28], LIHC 1.41 [1.08, 1.98])—whereas TT k_f was equal to or higher than NN k_f (e.g. LUAD 0.282 versus 0.226). The reversal therefore reflects a more stable housekeeping baseline among adjacent non-tumor specimens, not smaller functional-gene divergence of tumor cells (Fig. 5a).

To ask whether the ratio resolves biologically distinct tumor states, we stratified LUAD tumors by driver mutation (EGFR-mutant n = 61, KRAS-mutant n = 120, wild-type n = 311; double mutants, n = 2, excluded) using per-tumor ω (the mean over each tumor’s within-cohort TT pairs; Fig. 5b). KRAS-mutant tumors showed the highest tissue-level divergence (mean ω = 136.9), exceeding wild-type (115.4; Kruskal-Wallis across groups P = 7.8 × 10⁻⁷; Dunn–Holm P = 3.6 × 10⁻⁷) and EGFR-mutant tumors (122.2; Dunn–Holm P = 0.008), while EGFR-mutant and wild-type tumors did not differ significantly (Dunn–Holm P = 0.39; bootstrap difference 6.8, 95% CI −2.1 to 15.2). The k_f/k_n decomposition separated the two drivers’ associations mechanistically (Fig. 5c,d): the KRAS contrast carried a functional component (KRAS versus EGFR under k_f alone, Dunn–Holm P = 0.015; KRAS−WT k_f difference 0.011, 95% CI 0.002–0.019) accompanied by a lower k_n (WT > KRAS, Dunn–Holm P = 4.2 × 10⁻⁴), both of which raise the ratio. Because driver classes could differ in tumor purity, we scored every tumor with the ESTIMATE stromal and immune signatures and used the combined admixture score as a covariate (Methods): admixture differed across driver groups, highest in EGFR-mutant tumors (Kruskal-Wallis P = 0.003), and after admixture adjustment the KRAS–wild-type difference remained significant for both components (Δω +16.8, P = 4.0 × 10⁻⁶; Δk_f +0.012, P = 0.009; Δk_n −0.0004, P = 0.003), whereas EGFR-mutant tumors no longer differed from wild-type in any metric (all P > 0.4)—the apparent EGFR elevation is attributable to stromal/immune admixture rather than to a baseline shift. KRAS-mutant tumors were strongly enriched for ever-smokers (94% versus 63% in EGFR-mutant and 86% in wild-type tumors, χ² = 30.3, P = 2.7 × 10⁻⁷), yet adjusting for smoking status—alone or jointly with age, sex, and admixture—left the KRAS–wild-type differences essentially unchanged (Δω +13.9, P = 5.9 × 10⁻⁴ with smoking alone; +13.6, P = 3.3 × 10⁻⁴ in the joint model, with both components likewise retained; Section 3.13 of the Supplementary Information). That a single bulk-level statistic, read through its components, distinguishes a functionally divergent driver class (KRAS, robust to purity and smoking adjustment) from an apparent association explained by admixture (EGFR) is precisely the decomposition CKI is designed to provide—although these remain observational associations: the admixture score is an ESTIMATE-style proxy rather than a consensus-purity estimate (it is monotonically equivalent to published ESTIMATE purity, so regression-adjusted conclusions are unaffected), and pack-years data were too sparse to model (smoking status covered 427 of 492 tumors, 87%). Within-cancer-type stratification analyses beyond LUAD (LIHC Edmondson grade, BRCA PAM50) are reported as denominator-dominated vignettes in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Note 9; Supplementary Fig. 4b), because both orderings reverse under k_f alone.

Three controls bound the interpretation of the pan-cancer reversal. First, a four-panel marker composition check (immune, myeloid, stromal, epithelial) with sample-level cluster bootstrap found the pooled tumor-pair k_n coefficient essentially unchanged after composition adjustment (−1.2%, bootstrap 95% CI −4.1% to +2.6%; per cancer type −16% to +37%), so marker-measurable composition explains little of the pooled reversal, though as a noisy proxy it bounds rather than settles the question (Supplementary Note 8). A gene-signature purity sensitivity settles the direction: scoring every sample with the ESTIMATE stromal and immune signatures (combined admixture; Methods), per-tumor k_n correlated negatively with admixture in every cancer type (r = −0.21 to −0.42), so admixture can only weaken, not create, the TT k_n elevation—and restricting tumor–tumor comparisons to the low-admixture (high-purity) half of tumors increased the NN/TT ratio in all five cancer types (LUAD 2.46 → 2.86, 95% CI 2.41–3.45; LIHC 1.13 → 1.22, its CI still including 1, so the four-of-five CI caliber is preserved; Section 3.13 of the Supplementary Information). The reversal is therefore not an artefact of stromal or immune admixture. Second, all reported TCGA statistics were recomputed under a linear probability mapping (p_i = (TPM + 1)/Σ(TPM + 1), replacing the softmax-over-log2 map), and this linear re-computation is the authoritative caliber throughout: the reversal, its ranking, the k_n elevation, and the LUAD mutation gradient were preserved in direction and rank order, although the LIHC effect size is mapping-sensitive and quantitatively weaker under the linear mapping (NN/TT mean ratio 1.13 versus 1.31 under the softmax mapping; softmax-caliber values are archived as a sensitivity analysis in Section 1.7 of the Supplementary Information). Third, the k_f/k_n decomposition itself localizes the reversal in the denominator, which composition adjustment of k_n leaves intact. Remaining limitations are inherent to bulk resolution: TCGA “normal” samples are adjacent non-tumor tissues from cancer patients, not healthy donor tissue, so NN comparisons quantify heterogeneity among adjacent non-tumor specimens; RNA quality differences between tumor and normal specimens cannot be excluded; and per-tumor ω was not associated with overall survival in LIHC (Cox hazard ratio per SD 1.06, 95% CI 0.85–1.32, P = 0.59, adjusted for stage, grade, age, and sex; Methods), so the reversal is a descriptive property of tissue-state divergence, not a prognostic marker. We report all TCGA findings as tissue-level functional divergence at bulk resolution, pending single-cell or deconvolution-based validation.

CKI reframes transcriptomic comparison—from measuring absolute distance to quantifying functional divergence relative to an internal baseline—but this is better described as a recombination of established ideas than a conceptual shift. Reference genes have long anchored normalization and quality control in expression measurement: qPCR normalization by geometric averaging of validated internal control genes 31, and the constrained-variance property of housekeeping genes has been characterized at the genomic level 13,32. The idea that an intrinsic, gene-property-dependent noise floor can serve as a baseline has likewise been central to the transcriptional-noise literature 33–35, which showed that expression variance is reproducibly gene-specific. CKI’s specific contribution is to combine these ingredients—a constrained reference set, a functional panel, and their divergence ratio—into a per-comparison, design-testable index with an explicit split-half calibration and a design-matched permutation null, rather than to introduce a new concept. The key assumption is that housekeeping (HK) genes are under stabilizing selection that constrains their expression variance across conditions, making them a practical constrained baseline against which functional divergence can be measured. This decomposition is heuristically inspired by Ka/Ks analysis, but CKI is a heuristic index rather than a formal measure of selection: unlike Ka/Ks—where a shared mutation rate cancels mathematically, leaving a pure selection signal—CKI uses empirically defined HK genes as the baseline, lacking a comparable mechanistic cancellation. The empirical calibration (split-half equivalent populations; 50 replicates across six control populations) yielded a mean ω = 7.70 (95% CI [7.37, 8.02]; the legacy six-split estimate 6.67 is consistent with it), indicating that k_f systematically exceeds k_n even for identical populations due to identity-gene selection. We therefore use ω_cal = ω / 7.70 as the operational scale, interpreting ω_cal relative to the empirical equivalent-population distribution rather than against fixed cut-offs: values well below the calibrated baseline suggest functional constraint and values well above it enhanced divergence—not as claims about Darwinian selection regimes, and with the caveat that the baseline is dataset-dependent (Results).

CKI is a divergence index, not a classifier—and this is by design. Classifying cell types from transcriptomic data is largely a solved problem; CKI answers a complementary question: regardless of cell-type labels, how much functional divergence separates two populations, relative to their shared baseline? CKI and standard metrics answer different questions, and choosing between them can be reduced to a few practical rules. Use CKI when the question is relative: is the functional divergence between two populations larger than what their own housekeeping baseline varies by, within the same dataset and gene-selection scheme? CKI then offers (i) a normalized, interpretable scale (ω relative to the split-half calibration), (ii) higher discrimination than raw distances for strong perturbations (simulation AUC 0.80 for ω versus 0.72 for k_f, 0.64 for raw JS, and 0.58 for cosine—under the simulation’s HK-anchored neutral-drift definition and top-200 gene selection; Results), and (iii) a specificity-first screen: with a design-matched permutation null, a significant ω rejects label exchangeability under that null, and attribution to functional divergence further requires excluding library-level confounders (batch, donor, dissection order, RNA quality; limitations discussed below); drift on the anchor is the null hypothesis by construction, and functional signal on the anchor is invisible to ω. Before trusting fine-grained ω orderings, diagnose the denominator: if per-pair k_n itself lies in the extreme tail of its own null distribution, or k_n correlates with technical covariates (composition estimates, batch, RNA-quality proxies), report k_f (or SES(k_f)) with the design-matched null directly rather than the ratio. In the real data analyzed here, ω orderings were predominantly denominator-driven (brain class-mean ρ(ω, k_n) = −0.73; TCGA NN/TT inversion and the supplementary stratification vignettes predominantly denominator effects; the LUAD KRAS gradient as the k_f-stable exception; cross-organ cell-type ranking not reproduced under k_f-only; Supplementary Note 9), so the honest default for ordering claims is k_f plus a design-matched null, with ω reserved for its specificity-first property—rejecting HK-anchored neutral drift—rather than for ranking; the real-data neutral-drift calibration on technical replicates supports exactly this reading (ω misreported least among the continuous divergence metrics at every tier of the drift ladder while retaining sensitivity to genuine regional divergence; marker Jaccard was more conservative still but offers no k_n/k_f decomposition; Results). A real perturbation demonstration (Kang et al. 14; Results) supports this boundary within the limits of its lane-confounded design: IFN-β stimulation raises k_n itself 1.2–5.7-fold above the donor-drift level, and where it does so most strongly (CD14+ monocytes) the ω AUC for separating perturbation from donor drift falls to 0.55 while k_f retains 0.98. This makes explicit that the index presupposes anchor stationarity: when the anchor itself moves, cross-metric contrasts—not absolute ω values—are the reliable signal (Supplementary Fig. 3). Use standard metrics when any of the following hold: absolute distances with cross-dataset meaning are required (ω does not transfer across datasets or schemes—compare ranks only); the task is classification or clustering of cell types (a largely solved problem where normalization adds little); weak-to-moderate effects must be detected at atlas scale (CKI’s sensitivity is bounded by permutation resolution and multiplicity, as the brain analysis illustrates); or the analysis needs an orthogonal signal dimension (ω is a k_n-dominated composite correlated with the standard metrics, not an independent axis). When the baseline itself is suspect—bulk data with composition shifts, as in TCGA—interpret k_n and k_f separately (or use k_f-only orderings with composition controls) rather than trusting ω alone.

For prospective applications of CKI we recommend the following. (i) Cell numbers: use at least 100–200 cells per group; the calibration controls and simulation replicates used 100–200 cells, and below this range pseudobulk noise inflates both k_n and k_f unpredictably. (ii) Reference gene set: use the HRT Atlas v1.0 housekeeping panel (shipped with the package as cki/data/hrt_atlas.csv) rather than data-driven auto-detection, so that k_n is anchored to a pre-specified constrained gene set. (iii) Estimation: compare populations only within the same dataset and the same gene-selection scheme; absolute ω does not transfer across datasets or schemes (Results). (iv) Baseline: calibrate within the dataset of interest by randomly splitting the same population into halves and computing split-half ω with the analysis pipeline being used, and interpret ω relative to that internal baseline rather than the mouse-derived 7.70. (v) Inference: use a permutation null that matches the experimental design (block-shuffle where libraries or donors define blocks)—the reference implementation used here is provided in the package as cki.blocknull.block_shuffle_test—and expect bounded sensitivity: ω is a specificity-first screen whose value is rejecting neutral drift, not detecting weak-to-moderate functional shifts.

Gene-set definition, calibration, and estimator dependence. CKI uses the HRT Atlas v1.0 reference 13 for all reported analyses; the package also supports data-driven auto-detection (combined detection-rate and CV filtering, use_reference = False), not used here, and sensitivity analysis showed robustness to alternative HK definitions (the lowest 10% variable genes as a constrained set yielded ω correlations r > 0.95). Identity-gene selection for k_f introduces systematic inflation: calibration controls (random split of the same population) yielded a mean ω = 7.70, because pre-selecting genes by the observed group difference (DE scheme) or by high cross-cell variance (HVG scheme) inflates k_f even for identical populations; the permutation test accounts for this by constructing the null under the same gene-selection procedure. A further concern is the circular dependency of the per-pair k_f scheme—the top-200 genes are selected by |μ_A − μ_B|, so the genes defining functional divergence are precisely those with the largest expression differences—and although the permutation null preserves this selection, k_f magnitudes lack independent external validation and should be interpreted as an upper bound; the fixed gene-panel ablation (Results) confirmed that replacing the circular panel deflates absolute ω while preserving rankings, so absolute tier thresholds are scheme-specific. Housekeeping gene expression may be dysregulated in cancer 39, potentially affecting the k_n baseline in a disease-specific manner—one instance of the anchor-failure scope stated with the concept definition (Introduction). The empirical calibration factor (ω_cal = ω / 7.70, 95% CI [7.37, 8.02]) derives from mouse split-half controls (50 replicates across six control populations) under the per-pair DE hybrid scheme; the mouse full pairwise matrix (703 pairs, Supplementary Fig. 1) uses a different scheme (global top-2,000 HVGs), so cross-scheme comparison of absolute ω requires caution. Data- and design-specific constraints. TCGA analysis was limited to bulk RNA-seq resolution; the per-cancer permutation test (B = 1,000) permutes sample labels (tumor vs. normal) and holds the identity gene panel fixed across permutations, which is anti-conservative relative to per-permutation re-selection (Methods), and with 5 cancer types the TCGA results are exploratory. On the pair-level TCGA data (35,306 pairs) the kn_floor = 1 × 10⁻⁴ denominator floor was triggered by a single pair (1 of 35,306: k_n = 8.41 × 10⁻⁵, a KIRC NN pair; floor fraction < 0.1%), so pair-level ω is effectively k_f/k_n and remains a k_n-dominated composite (Spearman ρ(log ω, log k_f) = +0.04, ρ(log ω, log k_n) = −0.82; log-scale variance of k_n accounts for ≈ 72%); the saturation caveat applies only to the aggregate tumor-versus-normal analysis (aggregate k_n ranged from 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ to 1.9 × 10⁻⁴, so aggregate ω saturates at k_f/10⁻⁴ in 3 of 5 cancer types), and all single-cell analyses use the package default positivity guard only (kn_floor = 0), leaving k_n uncapped. The brain analysis uses post-mortem tissue; post-mortem interval (PMI) varies across samples and may differentially affect RNA integrity by region and cell type, potentially biasing regional ω comparisons—in particular, mitochondrial transcripts degrade faster post-mortem than nuclear-encoded mRNAs, so regionally differential degradation of mitochondrial and nuclear-encoded metabolic genes within the HK set could masquerade as regional structure in k_n; because the candidate screen is rule-driven rather than evidence of PMI robustness (Results), PMI sensitivity would need per-candidate evaluation—particularly for the thalamic and cortical dissections—and developmental time courses with controlled collection would provide stronger evidence for region-association inference. Donor-level PMI and RNA-integrity covariates were not available to us for this atlas, so a direct correlation between PMI and per-pair k_n could not be tested; such a check is the minimal sensitivity analysis required before any candidate-level region-association claim. In addition, the brain atlas is single-nucleus rather than whole-cell RNA-seq: nuclear capture depletes cytoplasmic transcripts, and if this depletion acts differently on housekeeping (k_n) and identity (k_f) gene sets—for example, because identity genes are enriched for secreted or membrane proteins—per-pair ω could shift systematically across classes; we did not model this nucleocytoplasmic bias explicitly. Relatedly, the brain class gradient is inflated by class-size imbalance: class-mean k_n is confounded with class nuclei count (Spearman ρ = −0.648, P = 0.043), and equal-n downsampling attenuates the endpoint gradient from 6.10-fold to 1.74-fold (direction preserved, magnitude attenuated ~70%; Results), so the headline magnitude—though not its direction—is a property of the unbalanced class sizes. Similarly, the +1 pseudocount added before the JS divergence acts asymmetrically on the two gene sets: k_n, computed on highly expressed housekeeping genes, is largely insensitive to it, whereas k_f, which includes lowly expressed regulatory genes, is more affected, and JS divergence on probability vectors is inherently dominated by high-expression genes (Supplementary Note 15 excludes dimension effects but not expression-level effects); the ‘functional’ reading of k_f should be tempered accordingly. A semi-synthetic ground-truth simulation (module-shift injection on a real single-cell background; Supplementary Note 1) validated the specificity of ω against neutral drift (type-I error 0.00–0.02, versus 0.55–0.58 for raw JS and cosine) but bounded its sensitivity: detecting injected module shifts was background-dependent—in the marrow background ω required δ = 2 (fourfold shifts on at least 200 genes), whereas the skin background reached 91% detection at δ = 1 (twofold shifts on at least 200 genes)—and ω ranked below raw JS and cosine in raw detection power while ranking first in functional-versus-neutral discrimination (AUC = 0.80). The simulation was restricted to two backgrounds (one cell type each: marrow B cells and skin keratinocyte stem cells), a single module-size series, and simple multiplicative perturbations; lineage-structured, gradual, or compensatory ground truths were not evaluated, and no head-to-head comparison against specialized cross-dataset integration methods (e.g., SATURN) was performed.

On behalf of my co-author, Dr. Xianming Wu (Chinese Institute for Brain Research, Beijing), I submit our manuscript, “CKI is a Ka/Ks-inspired index quantifying functional divergence in single-cell genomics”, for consideration as an Article in Nature Communications. CKI (Cell-type Ka/Ks-inspired Index) is, to our knowledge, the first framework to make a principled separation of functional divergence from background drift in transcriptomic comparisons: adapting the Ka/Ks ratio’s logic, it decomposes divergence into a housekeeping baseline k_n and an identity-gene rate k_f (ω = k_f/k_n). The work fits Nature Communications’ scope of computational methods validated on large-scale real data.

The manuscript rests on three pillars. First, in ground-truth simulation (1,750 replicates), ω alone did not false-trigger on neutral housekeeping drift (false-positive rate 0.00 versus 0.55–0.58 for JS and cosine) and ranked first for functional-versus-neutral discrimination (AUC = 0.80).
