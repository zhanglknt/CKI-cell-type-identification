Data- and design-specific constraints. TCGA analysis was limited to bulk RNA-seq resolution; the per-cancer permutation test (B = 1,000) permutes sample labels (tumor vs. normal) and holds the identity gene panel fixed across permutations, which is anti-conservative relative to per-permutation re-selection (Methods), and with 5 cancer types the TCGA results are exploratory. On the pair-level TCGA data (35,306 pairs) the kn_floor = 1 × 10⁻⁴ denominator floor was triggered by a single pair (1 of 35,306: k_n = 8.41 × 10⁻⁵, a KIRC NN pair; floor fraction < 0.1%), so pair-level ω is effectively k_f/k_n and remains a k_n-dominated composite (Spearman ρ(log ω, log k_f) = +0.04, ρ(log ω, log k_n) = −0.82; log-scale variance of k_n accounts for ≈ 72%); the saturation caveat applies only to the aggregate tumor-versus-normal analysis (aggregate k_n ranged from 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ to 1.9 × 10⁻⁴, so aggregate ω saturates at k_f/10⁻⁴ in 3 of 5 cancer types), and all single-cell analyses use the package default positivity guard only (kn_floor = 0), leaving k_n uncapped. The brain analysis uses post-mortem tissue; post-mortem interval (PMI) varies across samples and may differentially affect RNA integrity by region and cell type, potentially biasing regional ω comparisons—in particular, mitochondrial transcripts degrade faster post-mortem than nuclear-encoded mRNAs, so regionally differential degradation of mitochondrial and nuclear-encoded metabolic genes within the HK set could masquerade as regional structure in k_n; because the candidate screen is rule-driven rather than evidence of PMI robustness (Results), PMI sensitivity would need per-candidate evaluation—particularly for the thalamic and cortical dissections—and developmental time courses with controlled collection would provide stronger evidence for region-association inference. Donor-level PMI and RNA-integrity covariates were not available to us for this atlas, so a direct correlation between PMI and per-pair k_n could not be tested; such a check is the minimal sensitivity analysis required before any candidate-level region-association claim. In addition, the brain atlas is single-nucleus rather than whole-cell RNA-seq: nuclear capture depletes cytoplasmic transcripts, and if this depletion acts differently on housekeeping (k_n) and identity (k_f) gene sets—for example, because identity genes are enriched for secreted or membrane proteins—per-pair ω could shift systematically across classes; we did not model this nucleocytoplasmic bias explicitly. Relatedly, the brain class gradient is inflated by class-size imbalance: class-mean k_n is confounded with class nuclei count (Spearman ρ = −0.648, P = 0.043), and equal-n downsampling attenuates the endpoint gradient from 6.10-fold to 1.74-fold (direction preserved, magnitude attenuated ~70%; Results), so the headline magnitude—though not its direction—is a property of the unbalanced class sizes. Similarly, the +1 pseudocount added before the JS divergence acts asymmetrically on the two gene sets: k_n, computed on highly expressed housekeeping genes, is largely insensitive to it, whereas k_f, which includes lowly expressed regulatory genes, is more affected, and JS divergence on probability vectors is inherently dominated by high-expression genes (Supplementary Note 15 excludes dimension effects but not expression-level effects); the ‘functional’ reading of k_f should be tempered accordingly. A semi-synthetic ground-truth simulation (module-shift injection on a real single-cell background; Supplementary Note 1) validated the specificity of ω against neutral drift (type-I error 0.00–0.02, versus 0.55–0.58 for raw JS and cosine) but bounded its sensitivity: detecting injected module shifts was background-dependent—in the marrow background ω required δ = 2 (fourfold shifts on at least 200 genes), whereas the skin background reached 91% detection at δ = 1 (twofold shifts on at least 200 genes)—and ω ranked below raw JS and cosine in raw detection power while ranking first in functional-versus-neutral discrimination (AUC = 0.80). The simulation was restricted to two backgrounds (one cell type each: marrow B cells and skin keratinocyte stem cells), a single module-size series, and simple multiplicative perturbations; lineage-structured, gradual, or compensatory ground truths were not evaluated, and no head-to-head comparison against specialized cross-dataset integration methods (e.g., SATURN) was performed.
