Abstract
Comparing cell populations is fundamental in single-cell genomics, yet standard distance metrics conflate baseline variation with functional adaptation.
---
Inspired by the Ka/Ks ratio in molecular evolution, we present CKI (Cell-state Kinetic Index), which decomposes transcriptomic divergence into a baseline rate k_n (housekeeping genes) and a functional rate k_f (identity genes), with ω = k_f/k_n quantifying baseline-normalized functional divergence.
---
Calibration confirmed an empirical baseline for equivalent populations (mean ω = 6.67, 95% bootstrap CI [4.12, 9.33], all P > 0.05), inflated above theoretical unity by highly variable gene selection.
---
CKI ω was negatively correlated with all standard distance metrics (Spearman r = −0.39 to −0.47, all P < 0.001), indicating it captures an independent information dimension.
---
Brain analysis revealed a 6.9-fold regional differentiation gradient across 10 non-neuronal cell classes;
---
The ratio ω = k_f/k_n quantifies baseline-normalized functional divergence: ω near 1 means the observed differences are consistent with baseline expectation;
---
ω much greater than 1 means functional divergence exceeds baseline variation;
---
ω much less than 1 means strong functional constraint.
---
Importantly, CKI ω is a heuristic index, not a formal measure of Darwinian selection;
---
we use ω < 1, ω ≈ 1, and ω > 1 as convenient operational thresholds rather than claims about selection regimes (see Discussion).
---
First, we calibrated CKI on Tabula Muris mouse data (7), confirming that random splits of the same cell population yield ω above 1 (empirical calibration baseline 6.67, 95% bootstrap CI [4.12, 9.33]).
---
Second, we extended CKI to Tabula Sapiens human data (8) and found that CKI ω is negatively correlated with all four standard distance metrics, demonstrating that it captures an independent information dimension.
---
The analysis revealed a significant 6.9-fold regional differentiation gradient and a screen of anomalously similar cell-type/region-pair candidates;
---

Dimensionality invariance of JS divergence
Because k_n is computed on ~1,130 HK genes and k_f on 200–2,000 HVG genes, we verified that JS divergence is not systematically biased by gene set dimensionality.
---
A simulation of 2,000 random Dirichlet distribution pairs across dimensions ranging from 50 to 5,000 showed that mean JS divergence is effectively constant (0.155–0.159, ratio = 1.001 between d = 1,130 and d = 2,000;
---
This confirms that the systematic inflation of k_f relative to k_n (ω = 6.67 for equivalent populations) arises from HVG selection bias (selecting genes with high cross-cell variance) rather than from dimensional mismatch.
---
The calibrated omega (ω_cal = ω / 6.67) absorbs this bias into the empirical baseline, and the permutation null—constructed using the same gene sets—ensures internal consistency.

Bootstrap permutation test
We randomly permute cell labels between the two populations (B = 1,000 for all datasets: mouse pilot of 15 cell-type pairs and 6 calibration controls, human Tabula Sapiens, TCGA, and brain atlas), recompute pseudobulk vectors, and calculate ω_null for each permutation.
---
Empirical P-values are computed as one-sided permutation tests: P = (count(ω_null ≥ ω_obs) + 1)/(B + 1), where the +1 pseudocount avoids P = 0.
---
The one-sided test is appropriate because our hypothesis is directional: we test whether observed ω exceeds the null expectation (equivalent populations), not whether it differs in either direction.
---
Standardized effect size = (ω_obs − mean(ω_null)) / sd(ω_null), reported as a descriptive measure of separation magnitude.
---
Given the non-normal ω distribution, SES is interpreted as a non-parametric descriptive statistic complementing the permutation P-value, not as a parametric test statistic such as Cohen’s d.

Multiplicative residual model for brain regional analysis
For the brain regional analysis, we designed a multiplicative model to detect cell-type/region-pair combinations with anomalously low ω.
---
For each (cell_type, region_pair) combination, expected_ω = μ_ct × μ_pair / μ_grand, where μ_ct is the cell type's global mean ω, μ_pair is the region pair's mean ω, and μ_grand is the global mean over all 31,764 pairs (32.56).
---
We defined three confidence tiers: Strong (residual < 0.3, ω < 15, lowest ω in the region pair), Moderate (residual < 0.5, ω < 25), and Weak (residual < 0.75, ω < 35).
