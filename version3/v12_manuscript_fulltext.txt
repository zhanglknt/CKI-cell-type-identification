[P010|Heading 1] Abstract
[P011|Normal] Comparing cell populations is fundamental in single-cell genomics, yet standard distance metrics conflate neutral variation with functional adaptation. Inspired by the Ka/Ks ratio in molecular evolution, we present CKI (Cell-state Kinetic Index), which decomposes transcriptomic divergence into a neutral offset rate (k_n, from housekeeping gene expression) and a functional conversion rate (k_f, from identity gene expression), with ω = k_f/k_n quantifying selective remodeling. We validated CKI across four datasets: the Tabula Muris mouse atlas, Tabula Sapiens human atlas, TCGA pan-cancer data, and a human brain single-nucleus atlas totaling millions of cells. Calibration confirmed neutral behavior for equivalent populations (mean ω = 1.54, all P > 0.05). CKI ω was negatively correlated with all standard distance metrics (Spearman r = -0.57 to -0.38, all P < 0.001), proving it captures an independent information dimension. Cancer analysis revealed transcriptional convergence across genetically diverse tumors. Brain regional analysis identified 30 cell-type-specific developmental origin signatures among 31,764 comparisons. CKI provides a principled framework for quantifying selective transcriptomic remodeling, available as an open-source Python package.
[P012|Heading 1] Introduction
[P013|Normal] Single-cell transcriptomics has transformed how we study cells. Comparing two cell populations is one of the most common tasks: we want to know how different they are. Researchers typically choose a standard metric: Euclidean distance, cosine similarity, Pearson or Spearman correlation, or Jensen-Shannon divergence. These metrics are convenient, but they have a fundamental limitation: they treat all gene expression differences equally.
[P014|Normal] This matters because not all expression changes have the same biological meaning. A twofold change in GAPDH expression might reflect technical noise; a twofold change in a transcription factor might reflect a functional shift in cell state. Standard metrics cannot tell these apart. This problem is especially acute in large single-cell atlases, where donor-level and batch-level variation often dominates over cell-type identity. Methods such as Harmony [1], scVI [2], and SATURN [3] have been developed specifically to remove such nuisance variation. But after correction, a key question remains: how much of the remaining difference between two populations represents functional adaptation, and how much is simply neutral drift?
[P015|Normal] We realized that this question is reminiscent of a problem solved in molecular evolution forty years ago. When comparing two DNA sequences, the Ka/Ks ratio (also called dN/dS) distinguishes nonsynonymous changes (Ka, which alter the protein and may be shaped by selection) from synonymous changes (Ks, which are silent and presumed neutral). The elegance of Ka/Ks lies in its use of synonymous sites as an internal baseline [38,39]: the same mutational process produces both types of change, so their ratio reveals whether selection is acting.
[P016|Normal] Inspired by this logic, we developed CKI as a heuristic transcriptomic analogue. CKI defines two rates: a neutral offset rate k_n, estimated from housekeeping (HK) gene expression [4]; and a functional conversion rate k_f, estimated from cell-type identity genes. The ratio ω = k_f/k_n serves as a baseline-normalized functional divergence index: ω ≈ 1 indicates functional divergence consistent with baseline variation (empirically calibrated; see Results); ω >> 1 indicates functional divergence substantially exceeding baseline; ω << 1 indicates strong transcriptomic constraint. Throughout this paper, we use evolutionary terminology ('selection', 'constraint', 'neutral') as heuristic metaphors for transcriptomic divergence patterns, not as claims about Darwinian selection on gene expression.
[P017|Normal] We validated CKI across four scales, each testing a different aspect of the method. First, we calibrated CKI on Tabula Muris mouse data [5], confirming that random splits of the same cell population yield ω close to 1 (neutral behavior). Second, we extended CKI to Tabula Sapiens human data [6] and found that CKI ω is negatively correlated with all four standard distance metrics, proving it captures an orthogonal information dimension. Third, we applied CKI to TCGA cancer data [7,8], revealing that tumors are paradoxically more transcriptionally homogeneous than normal tissues. Fourth, we used CKI to analyze a human brain single-nucleus atlas [9], measuring how the same cell types differ across brain regions and demonstrating that CKI can detect persistent developmental signatures—including developmental origin heterogeneity, colonization route boundaries, and a single postnatal migration event—from adult transcriptomic data.
[P018|Heading 1] Materials and Methods
[P019|Heading 2] CKI computation
[P020|Normal] We normalize raw count matrices to 10,000 counts per cell and apply log1p transformation. Pseudobulk vectors are computed by averaging expression across cells sharing the same cell-type annotation, requiring at least 10 cells per group. Housekeeping (HK) genes are auto-detected from data using a combined criterion: detection rate > 0.9 (expressed in >90% of cells) and coefficient of variation below the 30th percentile among well-expressed genes (mean expression > 0.5). For human and mouse datasets, the HRT Atlas v1.0 consensus set (1,130 human-mouse shared HK genes) [4] is optionally used as supplementary enhancement (union with detected set). For any other species, detection is purely data-driven without external references. To validate the neutral assumption, we performed a sensitivity analysis by varying the HK gene set size from 250 to 1,000 genes (in increments of 250) and recomputed ω for all 4,851 Tabula Sapiens cell-type pairs. The coefficient of variation (CV) of ω across HK set sizes was < 13% for 99.2% of pairs, confirming that ω is robust to HK gene set composition and that the neutral baseline is stable.
[P021|Normal] For populations A and B with pseudobulk vectors ε_A and ε_B, k_n = JS(norm(ε_A[H]), norm(ε_B[H])), where H is the set of HK gene indices and norm is sum-normalization for non-negative single-cell data (softmax only for TCGA bulk RNA-seq). k_f = JS(norm(ε_A[I]), norm(ε_B[I])), where I is the set of the top-200 most differentially expressed genes per cell-type pair (Seurat v3 flavor) excluding HK genes. The choice of 2,000 HVGs follows the Scanpy default parameter and was validated by sensitivity analysis: varying the HVG count from 1,000 to 4,000 yielded ω correlations r > 0.97, confirming robustness to this parameter. ω = k_f/k_n. JS divergence uses the natural logarithm. Sum-normalization converts expression vectors to probability distributions for non-negative single-cell data; softmax normalization is used only for TCGA bulk RNA-seq data where negative values may occur from log-transformation. The stability of the ω ratio depends on k_n being bounded away from zero. Across the 4,851 human cell-type pairs, k_n had a median of 0.034 (range 0.0018-0.221). For these rare near-degenerate cases, we recommend reporting ω with a lower-bound k_n threshold (e.g., k_n ≥ 0.001) to avoid inflated ratios. HVG selection uses the Seurat v3 flavor (sc.pp.highly_variable_genes with flavor='seurat_v3') throughout. In practice, 56.3% of all ω values were < 15, and the method is robust for the vast majority of comparisons.
[P022|Heading 2] Bootstrap permutation test
[P023|Normal] We randomly permute individual cell identities between the two populations (pooling all cells from both groups, randomly reassigning each cell to a group of the original group size) (B = 500 for mouse calibration and exploratory analyses; B = 500 for mouse calibration), recompute pseudobulk vectors, and calculate ω_null for each permutation. This permutation procedure tests the null hypothesis that the two populations are exchangeable with respect to their expression patterns; significant deviations indicate that the observed omega differs from what would be expected if cell identities were randomly assigned. We apply a two-sided bootstrap test: Empirical P = (count(|ω_null - 1| >= |ω_obs - 1|) + 1) / (B + 1). Cohen's d = (ω_obs - mean(ω_null)) / sd(ω_null). For mouse calibration, bootstrap permutation testing (B = 500) was used to assess statistical significance. For human, TCGA, and brain analyses, standard statistical tests were applied without multiple-testing correction; all reported P-values are raw, uncorrected values.

CKI ω was computed for all same-cell-type cross-region comparisons (31,764 pairs total), using the hybrid scheme described above. [P029|Heading 2] Method comparison
[P030|Normal] We computed five metrics on all 4,851 Tabula Sapiens cell-type pairs: CKI ω (hybrid scheme), raw JS divergence (all genes), Spearman distance (1 - ρ), cosine distance (1 - cos θ), and marker Jaccard distance (1 - Jaccard index of top-200 expressed genes). Inter-metric Spearman correlations and cell-type classification ROC-AUC were computed using scikit-learn.
[P031|Heading 2] Multiplicative residual model for brain regional analysis
[P032|Normal] For the brain regional analysis, we designed a multiplicative model to detect cell-type/region-pair combinations with anomalously low ω. For each (cell_type, region_pair) combination, expected_ω = μ_ct × μ_pair / μ_grand, where μ_ct is the cell type's global mean ω, μ_pair is the region pair's mean ω, and μ_grand is the global mean (8.01). The multiplicative residual = observed / expected. A residual substantially below 1 indicates the cell type is far less differentiated between those two regions than expected from both its own global plasticity and the region pair's overall divergence. We defined three confidence tiers: Strong (residual < 0.3, ω < 15), Moderate (residual < 0.5, ω < 25), and Weak (residual < 0.75, ω < 35). Strong candidate signals were systematically cross-validated against the developmental neuroscience literature to assign each signal to one of four biological mechanisms: developmental origin heterogeneity (DO), embryonic colonization route boundaries (CR), compartmentalized developmental specification (DS), or postnatal cell migration (PM). The residual < 0.3 threshold for Strong candidates was calibrated from the empirical distribution of multiplicative residuals across all 31,764 comparisons: the 1st percentile corresponded to residual ≈ 0.40, providing a data-driven threshold for anomalously low values. The Moderate (residual < 0.5) and Weak (residual < 0.75) tiers correspond to the 5th and 25th percentiles, respectively. We note that these thresholds are calibrated on the observed data rather than a formal null distribution; as a sensitivity analysis, permutation-based thresholds (shuffling region labels within each cell type) yielded qualitatively consistent but not identical cutoffs (Supplementary Figure S5).
[P033|Heading 2] Clinical severity analysis
[P034|Normal] For TCGA clinical severity analysis, we computed intratumoral ω for samples within each clinical stratum using the hybrid scheme (global k_n from shared HK genes (note: k_n uses ~1,000 shared HK genes while k_f uses the top 200 DE genes per pair; the ratio ω = k_f/k_n is scale-invariant within each JS divergence computation, so the gene set size difference does not bias the result), per-pair k_f from top-200 DE genes). BRCA PAM50 subtypes were classified using nearest centroid (Pearson correlation) against the published PAM50 centroids [16,17]; 44 of 47 PAM50 genes matched to TCGA gene symbols. LIHC Edmondson histological grades [18] were obtained from cBioPortal (n = 288 tumors with both grade and expression data). LUAD mutation status (EGFR, KRAS, WT) was retrieved from cBioPortal (n = 492 samples). Between-stratum differences were tested with Kruskal-Wallis for PAM50 subtypes and LUAD mutations; trend across Edmondson grades was tested with Jonckheere-Terpstra. Paired vs. unpaired tumor-normal comparisons used Mann-Whitney U test.

[P039|Heading 1] Results
[P040|Heading 2] Decomposing transcriptomic variation
[P041|Normal] CKI takes two cell populations as input, each represented as a pseudobulk expression vector (the mean expression across all cells in that population). The computation has three steps, all of which use the same metric (Jensen-Shannon divergence) on the same underlying expression matrix, ensuring the ratio is internally calibrated (Fig. 1).
[P042|Normal] Step 1: Compute the neutral offset rate k_n. We restrict the pseudobulk vectors to housekeeping (HK) gene indices and apply softmax normalization, which converts expression values to probabilities. k_n is the JS divergence between these two HK gene probability distributions. Because HK genes, defined operationally by high detection rate and low expression variance, show empirically lower between-population divergence than other gene sets [4], k_n captures a practical baseline that includes technical variation, stochastic transcriptional bursting, and individual-level physiological differences. We use the term 'neutral' as an operational shorthand for this baseline component; HK genes may still be subject to stabilizing selection on expression levels (see Discussion). To assess the robustness of the CKI metric, we evaluated the stability of k_n ​across varying sizes of the HK gene set (250 to 1000 genes) for human datasets. The k_n ​values remained consistently low (ranging from 0.0147 to 0.0166) with minimal fluctuation, indicating that our CKI analysis is robust to the choice of HK gene set size (Supplementary Fig. S1A).
[P043|Normal] Step 2: Compute the functional conversion rate k_f. We restrict the pseudobulk vectors to identity gene indices—genes that define cell-type-specific functions. In the default configuration, identity genes are the top-2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs; Seurat v3 flavor), excluding HK genes to maintain k_n/k_f independence. k_f is the JS divergence between these two identity gene probability distributions.
[P044|Normal] Step 3: ω = k_f/k_n. For statistical inference, we perform bootstrap permutation testing (B = 500). Cell labels are randomly shuffled and ω recalculated to generate a null distribution. The empirical P-value is the fraction of permuted ω values that exceed the observed ω (with a +1 pseudocount to avoid P = 0), and effect size is reported as Cohen's d. Bootstrap inference uses B = 500 for mouse calibration. For primary analyses (human, TCGA, brain), standard statistical tests are applied without multiple testing.  The empirical ω distribution is right-skewed (median 13.68 vs. mean 14.12 for Tabula Sapiens); accordingly, we report median-based summary statistics throughout and use nonparametric tests where distributional assumptions are violated.
[P045|Normal] Decomposition of CKI into k_f ​and k_n ​revealed distinct regulatory profiles across functional pathways (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Notably, immune response pathways exhibited the highest fold change (k_f ​≈ 6.0) yet the lowest neutral divergence (k_n​ ≈ 0.15), whereas signaling pathways showed minimal dynamic range but maximal k_n ​(∼0.24). This marked pathway-specific variability prompted us to test whether incorporating predefined pathway enrichment scores could further refine k_f ​estimation.​ To address this, we ran a parameter sweep on Tabula Muris mouse data (703 cell-type pairs across 6 organs) to test whether adding pathway enrichment scores to k_f would improve performance. We found that the identity-only configuration (w_identity = 1.0, w_pathway = 0.0) achieved the best cell-type discrimination (AUC = 0.847, Supplementary Fig. S1C). CKI does not require external pathway databases to produce biologically meaningful results—partitioning the expression data into neutral and identity gene sets is sufficient.
[P046|Heading 2] Calibration confirms neutral behavior at baseline
[P047|Normal] We calibrated CKI on the Tabula Muris FACS dataset [5] (SmartSeq2, 15,057 cells, 22,308 genes, 6 organs). Housekeeping genes were auto-detected from data using a combined criterion (detection rate > 0.9 and CV below the 30th percentile), supplemented with 1,130 human-mouse conserved reference HK genes from the HRT Atlas [4] for human and mouse datasets. Identity genes were the top-200 most differentially expressed genes per cell-type pair (Seurat v3), excluding HK genes (Fig. 2). The neutral assumption was validated empirically: across control comparisons (random splits within the same cell type), k_n values were tightly concentrated (median k_n = 0.0019 (range 0.0006–0.0027)), confirming that HK gene divergence provides a stable neutral baseline.
[P048|Normal] The calibration confirmed the baseline behavior of CKI. We performed six control comparisons in which we randomly split the same cell population into two halves. The mean ω was 1.54 (median 1.42, range 1.09–2.10, 95% bootstrap CI [1.12, 2.08]), and none of the six comparisons reached statistical significance (all P > 0.05, two-sided bootstrap test). The deviation from the theoretical expectation of ω = 1 (mean offset +54%) likely reflects the operational nature of the HK gene set: our 'neutral' reference genes retain residual biological variation that biases k_n estimates conservatively downward, resulting in ω systematically above 1. An equivalence test (TOST, equivalence bounds [0.67, 1.50]) did not confirm strict equivalence at this sample size (n = 6), indicating that larger calibration studies are warranted. Importantly, this systematic offset does not affect the validity of relative comparisons (ω rankings and effect sizes), since all comparisons in a given analysis share the same HK gene set and normalization procedure. (Fig. 2A)
[P049|Normal] Beyond controls, ω values increased monotonically with biological distance (Fig. 2B). Same cell type across different organs (S category: mean ω = 4.03, n = 4 pairs) had lower ω than different cell types within the same organ (D category: mean ω = 13.18, n = 3 pairs). The component-level analysis confirmed that k_f was the driver: k_f increased roughly 1,000-fold from controls to inter-cell-type comparisons, while k_n increased only about 100-fold. This establishes that CKI measures selective remodeling, not just total difference.
[P050|Heading 2] CKI captures information that standard metrics miss
[P051|Normal] We extended CKI to the Tabula Sapiens human atlas [6] (108,136 cells; 6 h5ad files total, 99 cell-type entries, 6 organs: liver, kidney, heart, bone marrow, spleen, lung). For human data, we used a hybrid scheme: k_n was computed once globally (using the full gene-by-cell-type pseudobulk matrix with the shared HK gene set), while k_f was computed per pair using the top-200 differentially expressed genes for that specific pair. This hybrid approach keeps k_n on a consistent scale (all cell types share the same HK gene set), while k_f adaptively selects the most informative identity genes for each pair. Critically, since ω = k_f/k_n is a ratio of JS divergences computed from the same underlying pseudobulk expression space, the normalization remains internally valid despite the different gene selection strategies. HK genes were auto-detected (combined criterion, with optional HRT Atlas enhancement) (Fig. 3).
[P052|Normal] Human ω values ranged from 1.10 to 58.69 (median 13.68, n = 4,851 pairs), substantively higher than mouse (median ω = 3.63). This difference likely reflects both the larger number of cell types (99 vs. ~32) and greater donor heterogeneity in human data (multiple donors vs. inbred mouse strains). Despite this, the biological hierarchy was preserved: same cell type across organs (mean ω = 8.70, n = 60 pairs) was lower than different cell types within the same organ (mean ω = 16.18, n = 1,140 pairs).
[P053|Normal] The critical finding was that CKI captures a largely independent information dimension. We computed five metrics on all 4,851 human cell-type pairs: CKI ω, raw JS divergence (all genes), Spearman distance, cosine distance, and marker Jaccard distance. CKI ω was negatively correlated with all four standard metrics (Spearman ρ  = -0.57 to -0.38 , all P < 0.001). In contrast, the four standard metrics formed a tight positive cluster (pairwise r = 0.57–0.95) (Fig. 3B). This negative correlation is the strongest evidence that CKI measures something fundamentally different from all existing distance metrics.
[P054|Normal] CKI showed moderate cell-type classification performance (AUC = 0.716, 95% CI [0.698, 0.734], above Spearman distance at AUC = 0.690 [0.671, 0.709] but below cosine distance at AUC = 0.887 [0.875, 0.899]; Table 1). We note that this comparison is not strictly like-for-like: CKI's k_f uses 200 per-pair DE genes, while cosine distance uses the full gene set (~20,000 genes). When cosine distance is restricted to the same 200 DE genes, its AUC drops to 0.752 [0.734, 0.770], reducing but not eliminating the performance gap. This is consistent with CKI's design: by down-weighting shared HK gene patterns, it trades some ability to detect global transcriptional identity for enhanced sensitivity to functional specialization. Critically, CKI was the only metric where same-organ pairs had higher values than different-organ pairs (mean ω 16.18 vs. 13.77, Mann-Whitney U test, P < 0.001). All four standard metrics showed the opposite pattern (same-organ < different-organ). This reversal reflects CKI's sensitivity to functional specialization within shared microenvironments, a signal that standard metrics systematically obscure. We note that CKI’s AUC (0.716) and cosine distance’s AUC (0.887) are not directly analogous: cosine distance measures global transcriptional identity (including HK gene contributions), whereas CKI deliberately removes neutral variation to isolate selective remodeling. The lower AUC is therefore expected by design—CKI trades global classification power for functional specificity. The key insight is not the AUC ranking but the information independence: CKI captures a dimension (same-organ > different-organ reversal) that no standard metric detects. (Fig. 3C)Although CKI yielded a lower cell-type classification AUC than cosine similarity and raw Jensen–Shannon divergence, it was the only metric whose mathematical formulation permits full decomposition of pairwise similarity into neutral (expression-level, k_n) and functional (module-level, k_f) components—a property absent from all other compared measures (Fig. 3D).
[P055|Heading 2] Cancer analysis reveals unexpected transcriptional convergence
[P056|Normal] We applied CKI to TCGA bulk RNA-seq data across five cancer types (LUAD, LUSC, LIHC, KIRC, BRCA) [7,8], totalling 3,596 samples. We asked a simple question: when cancer develops, how much selective transcriptional remodeling occurs? For analyses spanning multiple cancer types, we applied per-cancer P-values are reported in Supplementary Tables for reference. CKI provides a principled answer by comparing tumor-tumor (TT), normal-normal (NN), and tumor-normal (TN) ω values (Fig. 4,Supplementary Fig. S2). Because TCGA profiles are bulk RNA-seq (tissue-level), ω values reflect sample-level transcriptomic divergence rather than cell-type-specific comparisons; interpretation focuses on relative patterns (tumor vs. normal) rather than absolute ω magnitudes. Additionally, because bulk profiles aggregate signals from multiple cell types, the observed transcriptional convergence (NN/TT > 1) may partially reflect convergent remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (e.g., similar immune infiltration or stromal composition across tumors) alongside malignant cell-intrinsic changes. Without cell-type deconvolution, we cannot fully disentangle these contributions. Future single-cell cancer atlas studies would enable more precise characterization of cell-type-specific selective remodeling. ω magnitudes, which are not directly comparable to single-cell-derived ω values.
[P057|Normal] The most striking finding was that tumors are more transcriptionally homogeneous than normal tissues. In all five cancer types, the median NN/TT ω ratio exceeded 1.0, meaning that normal individuals differ more from each other than tumors differ from each other. Breast cancer (BRCA) showed the smallest contrast (median NN/TT = 1.40), while liver cancer (LIHC) showed the largest (median NN/TT = 2.83), with intermediate values for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD 1.60), lung squamous (LUSC 1.43), and kidney clear cell (KIRC 1.98) (Fig. 4A-B). This convergence toward shared transcriptional states may represent common vulnerabilities across genetically diverse tumors. We further assessed whether this contrast arose from altered neutral vs. functional contributions by comparing ω values between tumor-normal (TN) and normal-normal (NN) pairs, revealing that NN values remained uniformly higher than TN values across all cancers (Fig. 4C). Bootstrapped Cohen’s danalysis revealed cancer-specific NN–TT contrasts, with large positive effects in BRCA (+1.04) and large negative effects in LUSC (−1.98) and LIHC (−1.22), while LUAD and KIRC showed negligible deviations (Fig. 4D). Tissue-level pairwise ω analysis across six organs revealed pronounced organ-specific heterogeneity, with the highest values observed in heart-lung comparisons (34.0)​ and the lowest in spleen-kidney comparisons (13.8), confirming that cellular similarity is strongly modulated by tissue origin (Fig. 4E).
[P058|Normal] Paired tumor-normal comparisons yielded higher ω than unpaired comparisons in four of five cancer types (paired/unpaired ratio = 0.99–3.25, Mann-Whitney P = 0.024 for LIHC, not significant for others). However, the small number of patients with paired tumor and normal samples (n = 2–5 per cancer type) limits statistical power and precluding definitive cross-organ cell-type conservation estimates.
[P059|Normal] Furthermore,to determine if the degree of selective remodeling correlates with disease progression, we asked whether ω tracks with clinical severity within cancer types. In liver cancer, ω decreased with increasing Edmondson grade [18]: G1 (101.8 ± 46.8, n = 39) > G2 (100.2 ± 63.9, n = 133) > G3 (96.8 ± 58.2, n = 105) > G4 (90.0 ± 57.8, n = 11; Jonckheere-Terpstra trend test, P < 0.001). In breast cancer, PAM50 subtype analysis [16,17] revealed a gradient of transcriptional heterogeneity: Luminal A tumors had the highest intratumoral ω (344.5 ± 323.4, n = 224), followed by Luminal B (313.6 ± 282.7, n = 123), HER2-enriched (263.0 ± 255.6, n = 55), and Basal-like tumors (223.4 ± 183.7, n = 97), with Normal-like tumors having the lowest ω (108.0 ± 65.5, n = 7; Kruskal-Wallis, P = 0.0002). Lung adenocarcinoma mutation stratification showed significant differences (Kruskal-Wallis, P = 0.017), with EGFR-mutant (285.3 ± 180.1, n = 61) and KRAS-mutant tumors (284.6 ± 227.9, n = 120) exhibiting higher ω than wild-type tumors (237.6 ± 195.4, n = 311).
[P060|Heading 2] Cross-organ cell-type conservation reveals a spectrum of cell-type functional constraint across organs
[P061|Normal] To disentangle cell-intrinsic identity from tissue-specific adaptation, we leveraged CKI to quantify functional constraint across cell types (Fig. 5A). Among the 4,851 Tabula Sapiens cell-type pairs, 59 same-cell-type cross-organ comparisons were identified (cell types appearing in ≥2 organs). We note that this analysis is exploratory due to small sample sizes: several cell types appeared in only 2-3 organs (n = 1-3 inter-organ comparisons), precluding formal statistical inference. Results for n < 5 comparisons are reported as observed values without standard deviation estimates and should be interpreted with caution (Table 2) .
[P062|Normal] Cross-organ ω values reflected known cell biology. B cells (ω = 2.70, n = 1, exploratory) and Neutrophils (mean ω = 2.72 ± 1.15, n = 6 pairs) showed the highest cross-organ conservation, consistent with their circulatory, environment-independent functions. In contrast, endothelial cells exhibited the lowest conservation (mean ω = 15.09 ± 6.46, n = 3 pairs), reflecting organ-specific gene programs tailored to local vascular needs [19] exhibited the strongest functional constraint, maintaining highly conserved transcriptional programs regardless of anatomical location. Smooth muscle cells (mean 6.29, n = 1) and Plasma cells (mean 6.61 ± 3.42, n = 6) followed, while Macrophages—the most frequently sampled cell type (n = 15)—displayed intermediate constraint (mean 9.84 ± 6.31). In stark contrast, Endothelial cells (mean 15.09 ± 6.46, n = 3) and Memory B cells (mean 16.83, n = 1) ranked as the most labile, reflecting profound organ-specific specialization.
[P063|Normal] The global distribution of pairwise ω values across all cell-type pairs further illustrates this asymmetry: the majority of comparisons cluster below the median ω of 6.9, with a long right tail extending beyond 25 (Fig. 5B). Cell types with low cross-organ ω consistently exhibited narrow, low-variance ω distributions, whereas highly labile cell types displayed broad, heavy-tailed distributions with extreme outliers (Supplementary Fig. S3). This intrinsic lability drives the cross-organ ω gradient, wherein Heart (mean ω = 24.3) and Lung (mean ω = 24.0) exhibit the highest mean ω values, while Kidney (mean ω = 19.0) and Spleen (mean ω = 18.9) remain comparatively constrained (Fig. 5C).
[P064|Normal] We note that several cell types (particularly those with n = 1 or 3 pairs) have small sample sizes, limiting the generalizability of cross-organ comparisons. These results are presented as hypothesis-generating observations that warrant validation in larger, multi-organ single-cell datasets with deeper sampling of underrepresented organs and cell types. Per-cell-type coverage is limited, warranting cautious interpretation of their absolute rankings (Table 2).
[P065|Normal] Crucially, CKI showed little agreement with standard metrics (Spearman r = -0.40 to +0.02, n = 60 pairs).
[P066|Normal] This discordance arises from CKI’s explicit normalization by the neutral baseline (k_n​): populations may share highly expressed genes (high raw similarity), but if their neutral baselines differ, even modest functional divergence produces a high ω. By decoupling functional signals from neutral noise, CKI reveals organizational principles of the cellular ecosystem that conventional metrics miss.
[P067|Heading 2] Brain regional analysis reveals cell-type differentiation gradients
[P068|Normal] We applied CKI to the Siletti et al. human brain single-nucleus RNA-seq atlas [9], which profiles ~3.3 million nuclei across ~100 brain regions. This dataset allowed us to ask: for a given cell type, how much functional divergence exists between the same cells residing in different brain regions? We focused on the 888,263 non-neuronal nuclei spanning 10 major cell classes (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursors, microglia, vascular cells, fibroblasts, ependymal cells, choroid plexus, committed oligodendrocyte precursors, and Bergmann glia), and computed CKI ω for all same-cell-type cross-region comparisons (31,764 pairs total) (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. S4).
[P069|Normal] The analysis revealed a striking differentiation gradient spanning 6.06-fold. Bergmann glia showed the lowest mean ω (2.37 ± 1.14, n = 21 pairs across 7 regions), followed by committed oligodendrocyte precursor cells (3.17 ± 1.47, n = 1,326 pairs across 52 regions), and fibroblasts (3.99 ± 1.90, n = 3,403 pairs across 83 regions). Vascular cells (3.40 ± 1.24, n = 3,321 pairs across 82 regions) and ependymal cells (4.13 ± 1.73, n = 780 pairs across 40 regions) showed similarly low divergence. Microglia exhibited moderate divergence (mean ω = 8.02 ± 4.93, n = 5,671 pairs across 107 regions). Oligodendrocytes and their precursors showed intermediate divergence (mean ω = 8.66 ± 4.44 and 7.65 ± 4.03, respectively). Astrocytes were the most regionally divergent cell type (mean ω = 14.36 ± 8.68, n = 5,778 pairs across 108 regions), a 6.06-fold increase over Bergmann glia (Fig. 6B).
[P070|Normal] This gradient aligns with known cell biology. Vascular cells and fibroblasts encounter relatively uniform extracellular environments across the brain—the blood-brain barrier and meningeal structures impose similar constraints regardless of anatomical location. Their low ω values suggest a conserved core transcriptional program with limited regional adaptation. Microglia, the brain's resident immune cells, showed intermediate divergence: while microglial phenotypes vary regionally, their core surveillance and phagocytic machinery is shared. Oligodendrocytes must myelinate diverse axonal populations and adjust internode lengths regionally, explaining moderate ω values. Astrocytes showed the highest ω, consistent with extensive literature showing that astrocytes express region-specific sets of ion channels, neurotransmitter transporters, and secreted factors tailored to local neuronal circuit demands. Notably, the ω heatmap across 9 anatomically defined brain regions​ reveals significant inter-regional divergence among astrocytes, with peak values reaching 11.2 between specific region pairs (e.g., A19–A25) that highlight pronounced functional specialization, whereas distinct low-ω clusters—particularly along the diagonal and within thalamic/hippocampal subregions—indicate transcriptional conservation consistent with shared developmental constraints. (Fig. 6C)
[P071|Normal] The cross-region ω gradient provides a computational framework for inferring shared transcriptional history between brain regions. Two non-mutually-exclusive mechanisms can generate low inter-regional ω: (1) recent or ongoing migration between regions, where insufficient time has elapsed for transcriptional divergence to accumulate; and (2) shared developmental origin, where sister populations inherit similar transcriptional programs that persist into adulthood. As our subsequent analysis shows, the majority of Strong signals are better explained by developmental origin than by active migration. Conversely, cell types that have stably resided in specific regions for long periods should accumulate regional transcriptional signatures, yielding higher ω. Under this framework, the low ω values for vascular cells and fibroblasts are consistent with continuous turnover and exchange through the circulatory and meningeal systems, while the high ω for astrocytes reflect long-term regional residence and functional specialization. Crucially, this approach supplies an orthogonal, transcriptome-based readout of migration history from static adult tissue, complementing insights obtained by direct lineage tracing.
[P072|Heading 2] CKI detects putative inter-regional migration events
[P073|Normal] Low CKI ω between a cell type across two brain regions indicates transcriptomic similarity beyond neutral expectation. We systematically cross-validated Strong candidate signals against the developmental neuroscience literature and identified three distinct biological mechanisms underlying low inter-regional ω: (i) developmental origin heterogeneity—cells from different embryonic progenitor pools (e.g., dorsal vs. ventral) retain distinct transcriptomic identities in adulthood; (ii) embryonic colonization route boundaries—immune cells that entered the brain through different developmental entry points show residual transcriptomic discontinuities; and (iii) postnatal cell migration—cells that physically relocate between regions through active motility. Mechanism assignment uses the multiplicative residual model to flag anomalous cell-type/region-pair combinations, with the biological interpretation corroborated by the known developmental biology of each cell type.
[P074|Normal] To formalize migration inference, we designed a multiplicative model: for each (cell_type, region_pair) combination, expected_ω = μ_ct × μ_pair / μ_grand, where μ_ct is the cell type's global mean ω, μ_pair is the region pair's mean ω, and μ_grand is the global mean (8.01). The multiplicative residual = observed / expected: a residual substantially below 1 indicates that the cell type is far less differentiated between those two regions than expected from both its own global plasticity and the region pair's overall divergence—a signature of shared transcriptional state potentially reflecting recent migration. We defined three confidence tiers: Strong (residual < 0.3, ω < 15), Moderate (residual < 0.5, ω < 25), and Weak (residual < 0.75, ω < 35)(Supplementary Fig. S5).
[P075|Normal] Among 31,764 cross-region comparisons, 30 (0.09%) were classified as Strong migration candidates: Astrocyte, fibroblast, microglia, oligodendrocyte, and vascular cells. Another 1,247 pairs (3.93%) were Moderate candidates, and 6,567 (20.67%) were Weak candidates(Fig. 6D). By cell type, the Strong candidate counts reflect a combination of regional adjacency, shared developmental origins, and ongoing cellular interchange.
[P076|Heading 3] OPCs: key negative control validates method specificity
[P077|Normal] Oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) are the most actively migrating cells in the adult CNS, continuously surveilling their environment along vascular scaffolds [20,21]. Yet CKI detected 0 Strong signals among 5,671 OPC cross-region comparisons—a finding that provides a critical orthogonal validation of the multiplicative residual model. Importantly, this result is not circular: the residual model uses each cell type's own global mean ω as the expected baseline, so a high-ω cell type like OPCs (ω = 7.65) would need to show anomalously low ω in specific region pairs to trigger a Strong signal. Among all 5,671 OPC comparisons, 93.6% (5,310/5,671) had ω < 15, yet none met the stringent Strong criteria (residual < 0.3, ω < 15, lowest ω in the region pair), demonstrating that the model requires more than just low absolute ω values—it demands deviation from cell-type-specific and region-pair-specific expectations. We acknowledge that OPCs' high global ω (7.65), reflecting their mixed progenitor/differentiated composition, raises the detection threshold: a Strong signal requires that a specific region pair's ω falls substantially below this elevated baseline. This could theoretically reduce sensitivity to genuine migration signals in cell types with high intrinsic heterogeneity. However, the 93.6% of OPC pairs with ω < 15 confirms that most region pairs already satisfy the absolute ω constraint; the absence of Strong signals is therefore attributable to the residual criterion rather than the ω filter alone. The model is not simply detecting high ω values or absolute transcriptional differences; it identifies cell-type/region-pair combinations where the observed selective remodeling is strikingly below what the cell type's global plasticity and the region pair's background divergence would jointly predict. OPCs have a high global mean ω (7.65) because their transcriptional program includes both progenitor and differentiation states; their 87 Moderate signals (residual < 0.5) and 1,002 Weak signals (residual < 0.75) likely reflect the balance between shared developmental origins and ongoing regional maturation [22]. The complete absence of Strong signals despite OPCs being the brain's most motile cell type demonstrates that the residual model differentiates between broad baseline motility and specific transcriptional signatures of developmental history.
[P078|Heading 3] Oligodendrocytes: developmental origin rather than migration
[P079|Normal] Mature oligodendrocytes contributed 10 Strong signals (residual 0.237–0.292), yet the prevailing view is that adult oligodendrocytes do not migrate between brain regions. We note that our initial analytical framework was designed to detect migration signatures; the developmental origin interpretation emerged from post-hoc cross-validation against the literature. We systematically cross-validated all 10 Strong signals against the developmental neurobiology literature. Strikingly, all 10 signals involved cortex/thalamus (A13/A14/A19/A32/A40/Idg vs. Pul/LP) or brainstem-internal (MoRF-MoEN vs. PnRF) pairings—precisely the anatomical boundaries between dorsal- and ventral-derived oligodendrocyte populations. Foerster et al. [22] demonstrated through dorsal oligodendrocyte lineage ablation that >90% of adult cortical oligodendrocytes are dorsally derived (from cortical radial glia), while thalamic and brainstem oligodendrocytes are ventrally derived (MGE/LGE precursors). Ventral-derived cells fail to adopt cortical transcriptional programs even when transplanted into the cortex, indicating persistent cell-autonomous transcriptional identity. Our CKI analysis detects this developmental origin signature: dorsal vs. ventral oligodendrocyte populations are far less selectively diverged than expected from the oligodendrocyte global ω (8.66), because their transcriptional differences reflect shared generic myelination programs rather than region-specific functional specialization. LP (lateral posterior nucleus) and Pul (pulvinar), thalamic relay nuclei, contrast with cortical Brodmann areas A13-A40, forming the most consistent developmental boundary detected by our analysis. This reinterpretation—that CKI Strong signals for oligodendrocytes detect persistent developmental origin signatures rather than migration—is fully consistent with Foerster et al.'s experimental data and provides, to the best of our knowledge, the first transcriptome-wide metric to distinguish dorsal and ventral oligodendrocyte populations without requiring lineage tracing.
[P080|Heading 3] Astrocytes: regional specialization with developmental origins
[P081|Normal] Astrocytes showed the highest global ω (14.36) yet contributed 6 Strong signals, all concentrated in thalamic subnuclei (VLN-VPL, CM-VPL, Pul-VPL, LP-VPL-MN), hippocampal subfields (CA2-3 vs. DG-CA4), and cerebellar lobules (CBL vs. CBV) (Fig. 6E). The thalamic signals are particularly informative: the ventroposterior lateral nucleus (VPL) appears in 4 of 6 Strong pairs, suggesting conserved astrocyte programs across thalamic relay nuclei that share a common developmental origin. Regionalized astrogenesis, driven by subnucleus-specific transcriptional programs, has been shown to produce persistent thalamic astrocyte heterogeneity that is detectable in adult tissue. Our finding that thalamic astrocyte pairs have ω values 5–6-fold below expectation indicates that these developmental signatures are selectively constrained—astrocytes in functionally related thalamic nuclei retain transcriptional similarity beyond what would be predicted from astrocyte global plasticity alone. The cerebellar CBL vs. CBV signal (residual = 0.274) reflects the molecular topographic zones of Bergmann glia and cerebellar astrocytes described by Reeber et al. [23]. Endo et al. demonstrated that Tcf4 controls astrocyte allocation during cortical development [24]; our results extend this principle to subcortical structures, showing that compartmentalized astrogenesis leaves persistent transcriptional signatures detectable by CKI across the entire brain.
[P082|Heading 3] Bergmann glia: cerebellar molecular topography
[P083|Normal] Bergmann glia had the lowest global ω (2.37) and only one Strong signal (CBL vs. CBV, residual = 0.274), consistent with their developmentally fixed, transcriptionally constrained state in the adult cerebellum. Bergmann glia are patterned into topographic molecular zones that align with cerebellar functional compartments [23], and their low global ω reflects their specialized role in maintaining Purkinje cell layer architecture with minimal regional transcriptional variation. The CBL (cerebellar lobule) vs. CBV (cerebellar vermis) signal likely reflects the established molecular topography difference between lateral cerebellar hemispheres and the midline vermis, rather than any migratory event.
[P084|Heading 3] Microglia: developmental colonization wave boundaries
[P085|Normal] Microglia contributed 10 Strong signals despite the prevailing view that adult microglia are maintained by local self-renewal with minimal inter-regional migration [25]. We interpret these signals as developmental colonization residuals—transcriptomic boundaries between microglial populations that entered the brain through distinct embryonic entry routes. Shemer and Jung [26] comprehensively reviewed the molecular determinants of microglial developmental colonization, identifying three major invasion routes: the pial surface, the ventricular zone, and the vasculature. Critically, colonization proceeds in a rostral-to-caudal wave, with forebrain regions colonized earlier than midbrain and hindbrain structures. Menassa et al. [27] confirmed this spatiotemporal gradient in human fetal brain, showing that microglial density peaks in the cortical subplate at mid-gestation before declining as cells redistribute. Barry-Carroll et al. [28] further demonstrated that microglial colonization occurs through clonal expansion of highly proliferative precursors, with spatial constraints limiting clone mixing between regions. Our CKI Strong signals systematically trace this rostral-to-caudal colonization gradient: 7 of 10 Strong microglia signals involve cortex (A5-A7, A14, A23, A29-A30) paired with midbrain/pontine structures (IC, DTg, PnAN, PnEN, MoRF-MoEN). The inferior colliculus (IC), a midbrain auditory relay, appears in 4 of 10 Strong pairs and we propose that IC represents a contact zone where forebrain-derived and hindbrain-derived microglial colonization waves meet. DTg (dorsal tegmental nucleus) vs. SN (substantia nigra), the single strongest signal (residual = 0.132), lies entirely within the mesencephalon—a region that receives microglial progenitors from both the pial surface and ventricular routes, potentially creating mixed-origin populations with shared transcriptional programs. This hypothesis—that CKI detects colonization wave boundaries from adult transcriptomic data—provides a testable framework for future developmental transcriptomic studies and suggests that adult microglial regional heterogeneity retains a persistent developmental signature from embryonic colonization history.
[P086|Heading 3] Vascular cells: blood-brain barrier regional identity
[P087|Normal] Vascular cells contributed 3 Strong signals, all involving ITG (inferior temporal gyrus, cortical) paired with brainstem structures (PB, PnRF, SN). Adult brain endothelial cells have a proliferation rate of ~0.4% and form a physically continuous network, making active migration an unlikely explanation. Schaffenrath et al. [29] systematically characterized blood-brain barrier (BBB) heterogeneity across brain regions, identifying hundreds of region-specific molecular differences in endothelial cells, pericytes, and vascular smooth muscle cells. These differences are established during development through region-specific transcription factors (Foxf2, Foxq1, Barhl2) that sculpt the BBB in response to local neuronal and glial signals. Our Strong signals likely reflect this developmentally fixed BBB regional identity: cortical (ITG) and brainstem vasculature have systematically different transporter repertoires, tight junction compositions, and metabolic enzyme profiles. The low inter-regional ω values (1.75–1.80) indicate that the core vascular transcriptional program is highly conserved, with selective remodeling detectable only as modest deviations from the global vascular ω baseline.
[P088|Heading 3] Fibroblast: the sole postnatal migration signal
[P089|Normal] Perivascular fibroblasts contributed 1 Strong signal (A40 vs. SN, residual = 0.299)—the only Strong candidate among all 30 that can be parsimoniously attributed to postnatal cell migration. Jones et al. demonstrated that brain perivascular fibroblasts originate from the meninges and migrate along penetrating vessels into the brain parenchyma during postnatal development [30]. This migration is active, coordinated with perivascular macrophage colonization, and establishes a population of fibroblasts in the Virchow-Robin spaces that persists in adulthood. The A40 (prefrontal cortex) vs. SN (substantia nigra) signal likely reflects fibroblasts that entered these anatomically distant regions along different branches of the cerebral vasculature from a shared meningeal origin. The brain-wide fibroblast population is small (8,156 nuclei across 83 regions), and the detection of a single Strong signal from this sparse population demonstrates the multiplicative residual model's sensitivity to genuine migration events with limited statistical power.
[P090|Heading 1] Discussion
[P091|Normal] CKI introduces a conceptual shift in transcriptomic comparison: from measuring absolute distance to quantifying baseline-normalized functional divergence. The key insight is that a low-variance reference gene set provides an internal baseline, inspired by—but not mechanistically equivalent to—the use of synonymous substitutions in Ka/Ks analysis. This decomposition lets us ask not just 'how different are these two populations?' but 'how much of that difference exceeds baseline expectations?' We use evolutionary terminology ('selection', 'constraint', 'neutral') throughout as convenient metaphors for transcriptomic divergence patterns; they are not intended to imply Darwinian selection on gene expression levels.
[P092|Normal] CKI is explicitly designed as a perturbation index, not a classifier; CKI answers a complementary question: regardless of cell-type labels, how much functional divergence separates two populations beyond baseline variation? The consistent negative correlation with all four standard distance metrics (Spearman r = -0.57 to -0.38) confirms that CKI captures information that existing approaches miss. Conceptually, CKI's k_n component relates to the transcriptional drift framework [37], which quantifies age- and disease-related stochastic expression changes. However, while transcriptional drift measures temporal accumulation of noise within a single population, CKI measures cross-sectional divergence between populations relative to a shared baseline—complementary perspectives on the same underlying phenomenon. While cell-type classification from transcriptomes is a mature field,
[P093|Normal] We emphasize that CKI is a heuristic, baseline-normalized functional divergence index, not a formal evolutionary selection model. The Ka/Ks ratio derives its theoretical power from a shared mutation rate (μ) that cancels in the ratio (Ka/Ks = f(N_e, s)), leaving a pure signal of selection; CKI lacks an analogous cancellation mechanism. k_n and k_f are computed on different gene sets (HK vs. identity genes) with different baseline divergence properties. Consequently, ω = 1 in CKI carries no population-genetic interpretation of neutrality: unlike Ka/Ks where ω = 1 maps precisely to neutral evolution (s = 0), CKI's ω = 1 simply indicates k_f ≈ k_n for the chosen gene sets. Our calibration result (mean ω = 1.54 from same-population splits) empirically confirms that the operational baseline is offset from the theoretical ideal. Moreover, in Ka/Ks, synonymous sites are mechanistically neutral by virtue of the genetic code; in CKI, HK genes are neutral only in the operational sense of showing empirically lower cross-population expression variance. These HK genes may themselves be subject to stabilizing selection on expression levels—their low variance could reflect strong functional constraint rather than true neutrality. We addressed this concern empirically: a sensitivity scan over HK gene set sizes (250–1,000 genes) showed ω CV < 13% for 99.2% of pairs, demonstrating that CKI's conclusions are robust to the operational definition of the baseline gene set. A formal theoretical treatment linking CKI to population-genetic models of expression evolution [38,40] remains an important direction for future work. In particular, deriving the null distribution of omega under explicit models of expression noise and stabilizing selection would provide a rigorous statistical foundation for the heuristic thresholds currently employed. We further note a key distinction in time scales: CKI measures cross-sectional divergence between coexisting populations (ontogenetic time scale), whereas Ka/Ks measures substitution rates accumulated over evolutionary time. The biological interpretation of omega thus pertains to cell-state differentiation and tissue-level transcriptional constraint rather than evolutionary selection on gene sequences per se. Connecting CKI to broader theories of transcriptional evolution—including expression noise buffering, stabilizing selection on gene expression levels, and the transcriptional drift framework [37]—would bridge the gap between the heuristic metric and formal evolutionary biology. Despite these theoretical limitations, the Ka/Ks analogy has proven productive in guiding the discovery of biologically meaningful patterns (e.g., cancer transcriptional convergence, brain developmental signatures) that would not have been apparent from standard distance metrics alone.
[P094|Normal] CKI complements rather than replaces existing methods. SAMap [32] and SATURN [3] excel at cross-species alignment; CACIMAR [33] provides conservation scoring. CellTypist [35] provides automated cell-type annotation at scale, scArches [36] enables deep-learning-based transfer mapping across atlases, and transcriptional drift analysis [37] quantifies age-related expression changes—each addressing different questions than CKI’s selective remodeling framework. Notably, transcriptional drift analysis [37] quantifies temporal expression changes within populations, while CKI measures cross-sectional divergence between populations—the two frameworks provide complementary views of transcriptomic variation. Quantitative benchmarking against task-specific methods (e.g., CellTypist for annotation, scVI for latent representation, kBET for batch detection) was beyond our scope but represents an important direction for understanding CKI's practical utility relative to established tools. More broadly, CKI provides a principled null model for any transcriptomic comparison: before concluding that two populations are meaningfully different, ask whether the difference exceeds neutral expectation.
[P095|Normal] The TCGA finding that tumors are more homogeneous than normal tissues (median NN/TT > 1.0) has implications for cancer biology. If genetically diverse tumors converge on shared transcriptional states, that convergence may point to common vulnerabilities that transcend individual mutations. However, we note that these bulk RNA-seq findings could partially reflect convergent tumor microenvironment remodeling (e.g., similar immune infiltration or stromal activation across tumors) rather than purely malignant cell-intrinsic convergence. Single-cell cancer atlas studies with cell-type deconvolution would be needed to distinguish these contributions definitively. The PAM50 analysis reinforces this: aggressive subtypes show the strongest convergence, consistent with proliferation programs overriding tissue-specific expression.
[P096|Normal] The cross-organ and cross-brain-region analyses establish CKI as a general tool for measuring functional differentiation at multiple spatial scales. The brain analysis revealed a 6.06-fold ω gradient across 10 cell classes (from 2.37 in Bergmann glia to 14.36 in astrocytes), demonstrating that CKI can detect regional functional specialization even among cells of the same type. Critically, the multiplicative residual model detected 30 Strong candidate signals and systematic cross-validation against the developmental neuroscience literature revealed that these signals predominantly reflect three distinct biological processes rather than active cell migration: (i) developmental origin heterogeneity—oligodendrocyte dorsal/ventral origin differences [22] explain all 10 oligodendrocyte Strong signals as cortex vs. thalamus/brainstem boundaries; (ii) embryonic colonization route discontinuities—microglial rostral-to-caudal colonization waves [26-28] create transcriptomic boundaries at the forebrain-midbrain interface, with the inferior colliculus identified as a candidate contact zone; and (iii) compartmentalized developmental astrogenesis and vascular specification [24,29]. Importantly, OPCs—the most actively migrating cells in the adult CNS [20,21]—yielded 0 Strong signals among 5,671 comparisons, providing a powerful orthogonal validation that the residual model specifically detects fixed developmental signatures rather than ongoing cell motility. The sole exception is the perivascular fibroblast A40-SN signal, which is consistent with known postnatal meningeal-to-parenchymal fibroblast migration [30]. Together, these results demonstrate that CKI detects persistent transcriptional signatures of developmental history—origin, colonization route, and specification—embedded in adult transcriptomic data, rather than inferring active migration per se. This reframing opens new applications for adult single-cell atlases as archives of developmental information.
[P097|Normal] Limitations should be noted. First, CKI currently operates at the pseudobulk level; single-cell extensions would need to address sparsity and dropout. Pseudobulk averaging discards within-population heterogeneity—a core advantage of single-cell genomics. The extent of information loss has not been formally quantified, though our calibration experiments (where pseudobulk ω correctly identifies equivalent populations) suggest that the aggregate signal is preserved for population-level comparisons. Second, the choice of the baseline gene set influences results. CKI uses data-driven auto-detection (combined detection-rate and CV filtering) as the universal default, with optional HRT Atlas enhancement for human and mouse. Sensitivity analysis showed that CKI results are robust to alternative definitions (using the lowest 10% variable genes as a neutral set yielded omega correlations r > 0.95). The CV < 13% stability threshold was chosen empirically; it is not derived from a formal model and should be interpreted as a practical guideline rather than a theoretical bound. However, HK genes, defined operationally by low expression variance, may still be subject to stabilizing selection on expression levels—their low variance could reflect strong functional constraint rather than true neutrality. In cancer, metabolic reprogramming (e.g., Warburg effect upregulation of glycolytic HK genes such as GAPDH) violates the stationarity assumption, and CKI results in tumor contexts should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The 'neutral' label is a practical approximation, not a mechanistic claim of evolutionary neutrality. Third, TCGA analysis used bulk RNA-seq data, which reflects tissue-level averages that include signals from multiple cell types, tumor purity variations, and stromal infiltration. Observed transcriptional convergence (NN/TT > 1 in all five cancers) may partially reflect convergent tumor microenvironment remodeling alongside malignant cell-intrinsic changes. Single-cell cancer atlases would enable more precise characterization. Computational deconvolution of bulk TCGA profiles using cell-type signatures could also help disentangle malignant-cell-intrinsic signals from microenvironment composition effects. Fourth, the brain regional analysis used only adult post-mortem tissue from a single species. Our 'developmental origin' interpretation of Strong signals is inferred from cross-validation with literature rather than direct developmental validation; lineage-tracing data or fetal brain atlases would strengthen this interpretation. The multiplicative residual model thresholds (Strong < 0.3, Moderate < 0.5, Weak < 0.75) were calibrated empirically on the observed data distribution rather than a formal null model; permutation-based null distributions generated by shuffling region labels (100 independent permutations) produced qualitatively consistent thresholds (Strong < 0.29, Moderate < 0.51, Weak < 0.76), confirming that the data-driven calibration is not circular. A formal null model based on parametric bootstrap or label permutation would further strengthen the statistical foundation of the residual-based thresholding. Fifth, the 30 Strong migration/developmental-origin candidate signals identified in the brain analysis have not been independently validated through orthogonal experimental approaches (e.g., lineage tracing, spatial transcriptomics, or functional perturbation). While our systematic cross-validation against developmental neurobiology literature provides support, these signals should be considered computational predictions requiring experimental confirmation. Sixth, the bootstrap permutation test (B = 500 for calibration; no bootstrap for primary analyses) may be underpowered for detecting subtle effects after multiple-testing correction. The minimum achievable P-value from mouse calibration bootstrap (B = 500) is ~0.002. For primary analyses without bootstrap, P-values are computed using standard statistical tests; individual pairwise P-values at the extremes of the distribution should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Future studies should consider B ≥ 5,000 for large-scale comparisons. Seventh, several analyses are limited by small sample sizes. Calibration experiments yielded a mean omega of 1.54 (rather than the theoretical 1.0), likely reflecting residual biological variation in the HK gene set and conservative bias in k_n estimation. This systematic offset does not affect relative comparisons but complicates the interpretation of ω = 1 as a strict 'neutral' threshold. An equivalence testing framework (e.g., TOST) with larger calibration samples is recommended for future validation. Similarly, cross-organ cell-type comparisons (Results: Cross-organ cell-type conservation) were limited by small sample sizes: several cell types appeared in only 2–3 organs, yielding as few as n = 1 inter-organ comparison (e.g., B cells), precluding robust statistical inference. These cross-organ results should be considered hypothesis-generating, pending validation in larger multi-organ atlases. Eighth, the hybrid scheme used in the Tabula Sapiens and brain analyses computes k_f from per-pair differentially expressed genes, which introduces gene-set asymmetry between k_n (fixed) and k_f (pair-optimized). This choice maximizes sensitivity but may systematically inflate cross-pair ω differences. our fixed-HVG sensitivity analysis on Tabula Sapiens (r = 0.72 between hybrid per-pair DE and fixed-HVG schemes) suggests that rank-order patterns are broadly preserved. However, per-pair DE selection systematically enriches for the most divergent genes between the two populations being compared, which inflates k_f estimates relative to a fixed gene set. Absolute ω comparisons across pairs computed with different DE gene sets should therefore be interpreted with caution; within-group comparisons using the same DE gene set are not affected by this bias. Using a fixed functional gene set (e.g., the union of HVGs across all comparisons) would eliminate this asymmetry at the cost of reduced sensitivity. Ninth, computational scalability has not been formally benchmarked. The brain analysis involves 31,764 pairwise comparisons; full pairwise bootstrap testing (B = 500) requires approximately 16 million ω calculations. While feasible on modern hardware (~8 hours on a 32-core workstation), scaling to million-cell atlases with hundreds of cell types would require algorithmic optimization or GPU acceleration. Tenth, the OPC negative control—with 0 Strong signals among 5,671 comparisons despite OPCs being the most actively migrating CNS cells—provides important validation of the multiplicative residual model. However, we acknowledge a potential circularity: OPCs were chosen as a negative control because their migration is well-characterized, yet the absence of Strong signals may simply reflect that the multiplicative model’s cell-type-specific baseline (mu_ct) absorbs the very heterogeneity that distinguishes migrating from non-migrating OPC subpopulations. The high global omega (7.65) suggests that OPC transcriptomes are constitutively heterogeneous across regions, which inflates the expected omega and raises the effective detection threshold—potentially masking genuine migration-associated signals. However, OPCs' high global ω (7.65) raises the effective detection threshold, potentially reducing sensitivity to genuine migration signals in cell types with high intrinsic heterogeneity. Complementary approaches (e.g., single-cell trajectory analysis, RNA velocity) would strengthen migration inference. Eleventh, we have not systematically analyzed cases of negative selection (omega < 1). The calibration offset (mean omega = 1.54) implies a systematic upward bias that may obscure genuine cases of transcriptomic constraint. Instances of omega < 1—which were rarely observed in our datasets—may reflect stabilizing selection on expression programs (e.g., essential cell-type identity genes under strong purifying constraint) and warrant dedicated investigation. Additionally, the omega distribution is consistently right-skewed across all datasets, which may affect the validity of statistical procedures that assume normality. Future work should explore non-parametric alternatives and variance-stabilizing transformations for omega-based inference.

[P111|Heading 1] Figure legends
[P112|Normal] Figure 1. The CKI framework. (A) Conceptual analogy between Ka/Ks in molecular evolution and CKI in transcriptomics. Ka/Ks uses synonymous substitution rate (Ks) as a neutral baseline; ω = Ka/Ks > 1 indicates positive selection. CKI uses housekeeping gene divergence for k_n and identity gene divergence for k_f; ω = k_f/k_n > 1 indicates selective transcriptomic remodeling. (B) Computational pipeline: raw count matrix → pseudobulk → JS divergence on HK genes (k_n) and identity genes (k_f) → ω = k_f/k_n. (C) Bootstrap ω distribution (B = 500 permutations) with median indicated by dashed line. Bootstrap ω values were simulated from a Gamma distribution (shape = 2.5, scale = 1.2, n = 1000). (D) Scatter plot of k_n vs. k_f, showing that functional variation dominates neutral baseline. Both k_n and k_f were simulated from Gamma distributions (kn ~ Gamma(1.5, 0.5); kf = kn × Gamma(1.2, 0.3)); dashed line indicates k_f = k_n. (E) ω distribution with ω = 1 (neutral) marked by dashed line.
[P113|Normal] Figure 2. CKI calibration on Tabula Muris mouse data. (A) k_n calibration across six Tabula Muris cell types from control comparisons (C category: random split of same population). k_n values are stable across cell types, confirming neutral baseline behavior. (B) Component decomposition of k_n and k_f across four comparison categories: C (same cell type), S (same sub-organ), D (different cell type within same organ), and X (cross-organ). k_f increases monotonically with biological distance, while k_n remains relatively constrained.
[P114|Normal] Figure 3. CKI captures independent information. (A) ω distribution comparison between mouse (n = 15 shared cell types) and human (n = 2000 pairs). (B)Spearman correlation heatmap of five metrics on n = 4,851 Tabula Sapiens pairs. CKI ω is negatively correlated with all four standard metrics. Standard metrics form a positive cluster. (C) ROC curves for cell-type classification across five metrics on Tabula Sapiens data.  (D)AUC (cell-type classification) vs. interpretability (decomposability) comparison across five metrics. CKI ω has lower AUC than cosine and raw JS divergence but is the only fully decomposable metric into neutral (k_n) and functional (k_f) components.
[P115|Normal] Figure 4. TCGA pan-cancer perturbation analysis. (A) Median NN/TT ω ratio across five cancer types. NN > TT in all cancers (all ratios > 1.0), indicating that normal individuals differ more from each other than tumors differ from each other. (B) ω distributions for normal-normal (NN) vs. tumor-tumor (TT) comparisons per cancer type. (C) Median TN/NN ω ratio (tumor-normal vs. normal-normal) across five cancer types. (D) Bootstrap Cohen's d effect sizes for NN vs. TT comparisons per cancer type. (E) Tissue-level pairwise ω matrix heatmap across five cancer types.
[P116|Normal] Figure 5. Cross-organ cell-type conservation. (A) Ranking of 38 cell types by mean pairwise ω across six organs (color-coded). Low ω indicates functional constraint; high ω indicates lability. (B) Global distribution of 1,406 pairwise ω values. The right tail reflects widespread functional divergence. (C) Cross-organ ω gradient (mean ± SD) showing systematic variation across organ pairs.
[P117|Normal] Figure 6. Brain regional cell-type differentiation and migration inference. (A) Brain region map schematic showing anatomical regions analyzed in the Siletti et al. atlas. (B) ω gradient across 10 non-neuronal cell classes. Bergmann glia (mean ω = 2.37) shows the lowest regional divergence; astrocytes (mean ω = 14.36) show the highest (6.06-fold gradient). Box plots: center line, median; box, IQR; whiskers, 1.5× IQR. (C) Astrocyte ω across brain regions, with hierarchical clustering. Cortex regions cluster together, as do thalamic and brainstem regions. (D) Migration candidate detection: multiplicative residual model identifies 30 Strong candidates (residual < 0.3, ω < 15, lowest ω in pair) among 31,764 cross-region comparisons. (E) Observed vs. expected ω for migration candidates, showing the gap between observed regional similarity and the cell-type-specific expectation.
[P118|Heading 1] Supplementary Figure legends
[P119|Normal] Supplementary Figure S1. Parameter sweep and pathway analysis. (A) k_n stability as a function of housekeeping gene set size, showing convergence at ~200-1000 HK genes. (B) k_f component contribution per pathway: decomposition of k_n vs. k_f for representative pathways. (C) Weight sweep for multi-component k_f. Identity-only (w_identity = 1.0, w_pathway = 0.0) achieves optimal cell-type discrimination (AUC = 0.847, n = 703 mouse cell-type pairs, 6 organs).
[P120|Normal] Supplementary Figure S2. TCGA per-cancer matrices. Pairwise ω matrices for five cancer types (BRCA, KIRC, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC) showing tissue-level transcriptomic divergence structure within each cancer cohort.
[P121|Normal] Supplementary Figure S3. Distribution of pairwise CKI ω values for representative cell types with lowest (top 3: lung leukocyte, kidney collecting duct cell, marrow granulocyte) and highest (top 3: heart fibroblast, heart endothelial cell, heart leukocyte) mean ω. Each panel shows the frequency (histogram) and kernel density estimate (line) of ω values, with min, max, and mean labeled. Low-mean-ω cell types exhibit narrow, constrained distributions; high-mean-ω cell types exhibit broad, heavy-tailed distributions with extreme outliers.
[P122|Normal] Supplementary Figure S4. Brain regional analysis details. (A) Cell type nuclei counts per brain region, showing the distribution of non-neuronal nuclei across sampled regions. (B) k_n/k_f decomposition per cell class, showing that ω variance is predominantly driven by k_f. (C) Mean CKI ω is significantly positively correlated with the number of brain regions (Spearman r = 0.74, p = 0.015), indicating that cell types spanning more regions tend to exhibit higher functional divergence.
[P123|Normal] Supplementary Figure S5. Developmental signature detection. (A) Multiplicative residual distribution for all 31,764 cross-region pairs, with Strong (residual < 0.3), Moderate (residual < 0.5), and Weak (residual < 0.75) tiers shaded. (B) Strong candidate counts by cell type. OPCs (0 Strong despite highest motility) provide key negative control, validating that the model detects developmental origin signatures rather than general motility. (C) Top 10 Strong candidates ranked by multiplicative residual, annotated with cell type, region pair, and biological mechanism. (D) Migration candidates by cell type and confidence tier, showing tier distribution across all 10 non-neuronal cell classes.
